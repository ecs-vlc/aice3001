* Keywords
  * Matrix completion, low-rank approximation, recommender systems,
    alternating least squares, non-negative matrix factorisation

* Main Points

** Missing Data and Recommenders
   * We are often given a matrix of data with missing entries (e.g.
     forms that people have only partly filled in)
     - most learning machines need complete data, so we either throw
       away rows or columns with missing values or try to infer them
   * We can only infer missing values if the matrix has structure.  The
     structure we use is that /structured data typically lies close to
     a low-dimensional subspace/ (the idea behind PCA and the SVD)
   * The best-known application is /collaborative filtering/: predicting
     the ratings users would give to items they haven't rated, from the
     ratings of all users
     - the classic method finds similar users and averages their
       ratings, weighted by similarity
       $$ \hat{R}_{ui} = \frac{\sum_{u'} \mathrm{sim}(u,u')\, R_{u'i}}
          {\sum_{u'} \mathrm{sim}(u,u')} $$
     - the simplest baseline uses means:
       $R_{ui} \approx \bar{R}_u + \bar{R}_i - \bar{R}$
       (user mean plus item mean minus overall mean)

** Low-Rank Approximation
   * The SVD of a complete $m\times n$ matrix is
     $$ \mat{X} = \mat{U}\,\mat{S}\,\mat{V}^\tr = \sum_k s_k\,\bm{u}_k\,\bm{v}_k^\tr,
        \qquad s_1\geq s_2\geq\cdots\geq0 $$
   * Keeping the $K$ largest singular values gives the rank-$K$ matrix
     $\mat{X}_K = \sum_{k=1}^K s_k\,\bm{u}_k\,\bm{v}_k^\tr$
   * /Eckart--Young theorem/: $\mat{X}_K$ is the best rank-$K$
     approximation to $\mat{X}$
     - in the Frobenius norm, with
       $\|\mat{X}-\mat{X}_K\|_F^2 = \sum_{k>K} s_k^2$
     - and in the spectral norm, with
       $\|\mat{X}-\mat{X}_K\|_2 = s_{K+1}$
     - if the data lies close to a \(K\)-dimensional subspace, the
       discarded singular values are small
   * *Completing a matrix with the SVD.*  The SVD needs a complete
     matrix, so we iterate
     1. fill the missing entries (e.g. with user means)
     2. compute the best rank-$K$ approximation $\mat{L}(t)$ of the
        filled-in matrix $\mat{X}(t)$
     3. copy its values into the missing entries only
        $$ \mat{X}(t+1) = \mat{W}\odot\mat{X} + \overline{\mat{W}}\odot\mat{L}(t) $$
        where $W_{ij}=1$ if $X_{ij}$ is observed and $0$ otherwise,
        $\overline{\mat{W}} = 1-\mat{W}$ and $\odot$ is the
        element-by-element product
     4. repeat from 2
     - in the lecture's example (a rank-2 matrix with a quarter of
       its entries hidden) the RMS error on the hidden entries falls
       from 0.95 (user means) to 0.22 after four iterations
   * More directly, write $\mat{X}\approx\mat{A}\,\mat{B}$ with
     $\mat{A}$ an $m\times K$ and $\mat{B}$ a $K\times n$ matrix
     - $\mat{A}\mat{B}$ has rank at most $K$, and has $(m+n)K$
       parameters rather than $mn$
     - $K$ controls the complexity; choose it by cross-validation,
       hiding some of the observed entries
     - fit by least squares on the observed entries $\mathcal{D}$
       $$ \min_{\mat{A},\mat{B}} \sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{D}}
          \Bigl( X_{ij} - \sum_{k=1}^K A_{ik}\,B_{kj}\Bigr)^2 $$
     - *LoRA.*  The same parameter count makes fine-tuning large
       neural networks cheap.  Low-rank adaptation (LoRA; Hu et al.,
       2021) freezes a pretrained weight matrix $\mat{W}_0$ ($m\times n$)
       and learns only a low-rank change
       $\Delta\mat{W} = \mat{A}\mat{B}$ with $K\ll m,n$, one factor
       starting at zero so that training starts from $\mat{W}_0$.  For
       $m=n=4096$ and $K=8$ that is about $6.6\times10^4$ trainable
       parameters instead of $1.7\times10^7$.  The assumption is that
       the change a new task needs is close to low rank.  Each task then
       stores only its own small $\mat{A}$ and $\mat{B}$, and after
       training $\mat{A}\mat{B}$ can be added into $\mat{W}_0$, so the
       adapted network is no slower to run
     - $\mat{A}$ and $\mat{B}$ are never unique:
       $\mat{A}\mat{B} = (\mat{A}\mat{G})(\mat{G}^{-1}\mat{B})$ for any
       invertible $K\times K$ matrix $\mat{G}$

** Probabilistic Approach
   * *Bayes' rule and MAP.*  For parameters $\bm{\theta}$ and data
     $\mathcal{D}$ the posterior is
     $$ \Prob{\bm{\theta}|\mathcal{D}} =
        \frac{\Prob{\mathcal{D}|\bm{\theta}}\,\Prob{\bm{\theta}}}{\Prob{\mathcal{D}}} $$
     (likelihood times prior, divided by the evidence).  The /maximum a
     posteriori/ (MAP) solution is the most probable $\bm{\theta}$,
     $$ \bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{MAP}} = \mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}_{\bm{\theta}}
        \Bigl(\log\Prob{\mathcal{D}|\bm{\theta}} + \log\Prob{\bm{\theta}}\Bigr) $$
     since the evidence does not depend on $\bm{\theta}$.  With a flat
     prior it is the maximum-likelihood solution
     - MAP keeps a single point and throws away our uncertainty about
       $\bm{\theta}$; a full Bayesian treatment would average
       predictions over the posterior
     - the peak of a probability density is not invariant: change
       variables (e.g. from $\sigma$ to $\log\sigma$) and the peak
       moves, although the probabilities do not
     - in high dimensions most of the posterior probability can lie
       far from the peak, so the MAP point can be untypical
   * Treat the ratings as noisy: $X_{ij} \sim
     \mathcal{N}\bigl(\sum_k A_{ik}B_{kj},\, \sigma^2\bigr)$, with
     Gaussian priors $A_{ik}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_A^2)$ and
     $B_{kj}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_B^2)$
   * The full posterior is intractable, but the /maximum a posteriori/
     (MAP) solution maximises
     $$ \mathcal{E} = -\frac{1}{2\sigma^2} \sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{D}}
        \Bigl( X_{ij} - \sum_{k=1}^K A_{ik}\,B_{kj}\Bigr)^2 -
        \frac{1}{2\sigma_A^2}\sum_{i,k} A_{ik}^2 -
        \frac{1}{2\sigma_B^2}\sum_{k,j} B_{kj}^2 + \text{const} $$
   * *Each step is ridge regression.*  Fix $\mat{B}$ and write $\bm{a}_i$
     for row $i$ of $\mat{A}$ and $\bm{b}_j$ for column $j$ of
     $\mat{B}$.  Then $\bm{a}_i$ minimises
     $$ \sum_{j|(i,j)\in\mathcal{D}} \bigl(X_{ij} - \bm{a}_i^\tr\bm{b}_j\bigr)^2 +
        \eta\,\|\bm{a}_i\|^2, \qquad \eta = \frac{\sigma^2}{\sigma_A^2} $$
     which is ridge regression with features $\bm{b}_j$ and targets
     $X_{ij}$.  Its solution is
     $$ \bm{a}_i = \Bigl(\eta\,\mat{I} + \sum_j \bm{b}_j\bm{b}_j^\tr\Bigr)^{-1}
        \sum_j \bm{b}_j\,X_{ij} $$
     (sums over the $j$ with $(i,j)\in\mathcal{D}$): one $K\times K$
     linear solve per row
   * Solving for $\mat{B}$ with $\mat{A}$ fixed is the same with the
     roles swapped.  Alternating the two is /alternating least squares/
     (ALS)
     - each step is a convex problem and cannot decrease
       $\mathcal{E}$, so ALS converges, usually quickly
     - but the whole problem is not convex, so it converges to a local
       optimum that can depend on the starting point
   * *Embeddings.*  Row $i$ of $\mat{A}$ is a \(K\)-dimensional vector for
     user $i$ and column $j$ of $\mat{B}$ a vector for item $j$; the
     predicted rating is their inner product.  Modern recommenders
     learn such embeddings with neural networks, mostly from /implicit
     feedback/ (clicks, views, purchases), where a missing entry is weak
     evidence of not liking rather than simply unknown
   * Matrix factorisation was central to the winning entries of the
     Netflix Prize (2006--09) and remains a strong baseline

** Non-Negative Matrix Factorisation
   * Factorisation is also a way of modelling data.  For a words
     $\times$ books count matrix, the rank-3 SVD gives factors with
     mixed signs, which are hard to interpret
   * /Non-negative matrix factorisation/ (NMF) minimises
     $\|\mat{X}-\mat{A}\mat{B}\|_F^2$ with every element of $\mat{A}$ and
     $\mat{B}$ non-negative
     - projected gradient descent: take a gradient step, then set
       negative elements to zero
     - or Lee and Seung's multiplicative updates ($\oslash$ is
       element-by-element division)
       $$ \mat{B} \leftarrow \mat{B}\odot\bigl(\mat{A}^\tr\mat{X}\bigr)
          \oslash\bigl(\mat{A}^\tr\mat{A}\mat{B}\bigr), \qquad
          \mat{A} \leftarrow \mat{A}\odot\bigl(\mat{X}\mat{B}^\tr\bigr)
          \oslash\bigl(\mat{A}\mat{B}\mat{B}^\tr\bigr) $$
       which never make an element negative
     - in the lecture's example the three factors pick out
       mathematics, physics and biology
   * Caveats
     - NMF cannot fit better than the truncated SVD, which is the best
       rank-$K$ fit (Eckart--Young).  In the example the relative
       errors are 0.164 (NMF) and 0.158 (SVD)
     - the problem is not convex, so the result depends on the
       starting point and on $K$
     - the factors are not unique, and a "parts-based" interpretation
       of them is not guaranteed
     - so compare against simple baselines such as the SVD or PCA

     numbers does a rank $K=50$ factorisation store, compared with the

** The prior fixes the scale
   * Show that replacing $\mat{A}\to c\,\mat{A}$ and
     $\mat{B}\to\mat{B}/c$ (for $c>0$) leaves the likelihood unchanged.
   * Show that the prior terms in $\mathcal{E}$ are then
     $-\alpha\,c^2-\beta/c^2$ with
     $\alpha = \sum_{i,k}A_{ik}^2/(2\sigma_A^2)$ and
     $\beta = \sum_{k,j}B_{kj}^2/(2\sigma_B^2)$, and find the $c$ that
     maximises them.  What does this say about the sizes of $\mat{A}$
     and $\mat{B}$ at the MAP solution?

** Ridge regression for one row
   * Differentiate
     $\sum_{j} (X_{ij} - \bm{a}_i^\tr\bm{b}_j)^2 + \eta\,\|\bm{a}_i\|^2$
     with respect to $\bm{a}_i$ and derive the update for $\bm{a}_i$
     given above.

** Multiplicative updates and KKT
   * Show that if every element of $\mat{A}$, $\mat{B}$ and $\mat{X}$
     is positive, the multiplicative update for $\mat{B}$ keeps
     $\mat{B}$ positive.
   * At a fixed point of the update show that for every element
     either $B_{kj}=0$ or $\bigl(\mat{A}^\tr\mat{A}\mat{B} -
     \mat{A}^\tr\mat{X}\bigr)_{kj} = 0$.  Relate this to the
     complementary slackness condition in the KKT conditions for
     minimising $E$ subject to $B_{kj}\geq0$ (the gradient of $E$ with
     respect to $\mat{B}$ is $2(\mat{A}^\tr\mat{A}\mat{B} -
     \mat{A}^\tr\mat{X})$).

* Experiments

** Alternating least squares
   * Make a noisy rank-3 matrix, observe 30% of its entries, hold some
     of those back, and fit by ALS for several $K$ and $\eta$
#+BEGIN_SRC python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
m, n, K_true = 60, 40, 3
X_true = rng.standard_normal((m, K_true)) @ rng.standard_normal((K_true, n))
X = X_true + 0.3 * rng.standard_normal((m, n))     # noisy ratings
observed = rng.random((m, n)) < 0.3                  # see 30% of entries
train = observed & (rng.random((m, n)) < 0.8)        # hold some back
test = observed & ~train


def als(X, W, K, eta, iters=50):
    """Alternating least squares: each step is ridge regression."""
    A = 0.1 * rng.standard_normal((X.shape[0], K))
    B = 0.1 * rng.standard_normal((K, X.shape[1]))
    for _ in range(iters):
        for i in range(X.shape[0]):           # row i of A, B fixed
            Bi = B[:, W[i]]
            A[i] = np.linalg.solve(eta * np.eye(K) + Bi @ Bi.T, Bi @ X[i, W[i]])
        for j in range(X.shape[1]):           # column j of B, A fixed
            Aj = A[W[:, j]]
            B[:, j] = np.linalg.solve(eta * np.eye(K) + Aj.T @ Aj, Aj.T @ X[W[:, j], j])
    return A, B


def rmse(P, mask):
    return np.sqrt(np.mean((P - X)[mask] ** 2))


mean = X[train].mean()
print(f"predict the mean:  test RMSE {rmse(np.full_like(X, mean), test):.3f}")
for K in [1, 2, 3, 5, 10]:
    for eta in [0.01, 1.0]:
        A, B = als(X, train, K, eta)
        print(f"K={K:2d} eta={eta:4}:  train {rmse(A @ B, train):.3f}  test {rmse(A @ B, test):.3f}")
#+END_SRC
   * What happens to the training and test errors as $K$ grows?  What
     does the regulariser $\eta$ do?  (See the answers.)

* Answers

** Counting parameters
   * $(m+n)K = 1.1\times10^6\times50 = 5.5\times10^7$ numbers, against
     $mn = 10^{11}$ for the full matrix: about 1800 times fewer

** Eckart--Young
   * Rank 1: Frobenius error $\sqrt{3^2+1^2}=\sqrt{10}\approx3.16$,
     spectral error $3$
   * Rank 2: Frobenius error $1$, spectral error $1$

** The prior fixes the scale
   * $(c\mat{A})(\mat{B}/c)=\mat{A}\mat{B}$, so the likelihood term is
     unchanged
   * The prior terms become $-\alpha c^2 - \beta/c^2$.  Setting the
     derivative $-2\alpha c + 2\beta/c^3$ to zero gives
     $c^4 = \beta/\alpha$
   * At the MAP solution $c=1$, so $\alpha=\beta$: the prior balances
     the sizes, $\sum A_{ik}^2/\sigma_A^2 = \sum B_{kj}^2/\sigma_B^2$.
     The prior removes the scaling freedom, but not rotations:
     $\mat{A}\to\mat{A}\mat{Q}$, $\mat{B}\to\mat{Q}^\tr\mat{B}$ with
     $\mat{Q}$ orthogonal changes neither $\mat{A}\mat{B}$ nor the
     sums of squares

** Ridge regression for one row
   * The gradient is
     $-2\sum_j \bm{b}_j(X_{ij}-\bm{b}_j^\tr\bm{a}_i) + 2\eta\,\bm{a}_i$.
     Setting it to zero,
     $\bigl(\eta\,\mat{I}+\sum_j\bm{b}_j\bm{b}_j^\tr\bigr)\bm{a}_i =
     \sum_j\bm{b}_j X_{ij}$, which is $\mat{M}^i\bm{a}^i=\bm{c}^i$ in the
     lecture

** Multiplicative updates and KKT
   * Every element of $\mat{A}^\tr\mat{X}$ and
     $\mat{A}^\tr\mat{A}\mat{B}$ is a sum of products of positive
     numbers, so positive.  The update multiplies each $B_{kj}$ by the
     positive ratio of the two, so $B_{kj}$ stays positive
   * At a fixed point
     $B_{kj} = B_{kj}\,(\mat{A}^\tr\mat{X})_{kj}/(\mat{A}^\tr\mat{A}\mat{B})_{kj}$,
     so $B_{kj}\bigl((\mat{A}^\tr\mat{A}\mat{B})_{kj} -
     (\mat{A}^\tr\mat{X})_{kj}\bigr) = 0$
   * This is $B_{kj}\,\partial E/\partial B_{kj} = 0$: either the
     constraint is active ($B_{kj}=0$) or the gradient component
     vanishes, as complementary slackness requires.  (A full KKT point
     also needs $\partial E/\partial B_{kj}\geq0$ where $B_{kj}=0$,
     which the fixed-point condition alone does not guarantee)

** Alternating least squares
   * Running the code gives (test RMSE; predicting the mean gives 1.67)

     | $K$ | $\eta=0.01$ | $\eta=1$ |
     |-----+-------------+----------|
     |   1 |       1.163 |    1.185 |
     |   2 |       1.116 |    1.075 |
     |   3 |       4.324 |    0.695 |
     |   5 |       2.286 |    0.898 |
     |  10 |       1.884 |    0.947 |
   * The training error falls steadily with $K$ (to 0.006 for $K=10$,
     $\eta=0.01$), but the test error is best at the true rank, $K=3$,
     with regularisation
   * Without regularisation the larger models over-fit badly: there
     are only about 10 training entries per row and 14 per column to
     fit $K$ parameters each
   * The test error cannot reach the noise level (0.3) because so few
     entries are observed
