* Keywords
  * Covariance matrices, positive semi-definite, dimensionality reduction, PCA, reconstruction error, duality

* Main Points

** Why Reduce Dimensions?
   * Data often varies significantly in only a few directions, even
     when it has many features.  You cannot usually see this by
     looking at the numbers
   * Projecting onto the few directions of greatest variation gives
     fewer, more informative features for a learning machine, and
     discards directions that are mostly noise

** Notation
   * We have $m$ data points $\bm{x}_1,\ldots,\bm{x}_m$, each with $p$
     features, and sample mean $\bm{\mu} = \frac{1}{m}\sum_k \bm{x}_k$
   * In this lecture we collect the /centred/ data as the columns of a
     $p\times m$ matrix
     \begin{align*}
       \mat{X} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{m-1}}\left( \strut \bm{x}_1-\bm{\mu},
       \bm{x}_2-\bm{\mu}, \cdots, \bm{x}_m-\bm{\mu} \right)
     \end{align*}
     This is the transpose of the design matrix used in linear
     regression (one row per data point), and it includes the
     factor $1/\sqrt{m-1}$

** The Direction of Maximum Variation
   * The spread of the data along a unit vector $\bm{v}$
     ($\len{\bm{v}}=1$) is the variance of the projections
     $\bm{v}^\tr(\bm{x}_k-\bm{\mu})$
     \begin{align*}
       \sigma^2(\bm{v}) = \frac{1}{m-1}\sum_{k=1}^m
       \left(\bm{v}^\tr(\bm{x}_k-\bm{\mu})\right)^2
       = \bm{v}^\tr\mat{C}\,\bm{v}
     \end{align*}
     where, writing $\left(\bm{v}^\tr\bm{a}\right)^2 =
     \bm{v}^\tr\bm{a}\,\bm{a}^\tr\bm{v}$ and taking $\bm{v}$ outside the
     sum, $\mat{C}$ is the /covariance matrix/
     \begin{align*}
       \mat{C} = \frac{1}{m-1}\sum_{k=1}^m (\bm{x}_k-\bm{\mu})\,(\bm{x}_k-\bm{\mu})^\tr
       = \mat{X}\,\mat{X}^\tr
     \end{align*}
   * To maximise $\bm{v}^\tr\mat{C}\,\bm{v}$ subject to
     $\bm{v}^\tr\bm{v}=1$ we use a Lagrange multiplier.  Setting the
     gradient of
     $\mathcal{L} = \bm{v}^\tr\mat{C}\,\bm{v} - \lambda\,(\bm{v}^\tr\bm{v}-1)$
     to zero gives
     \begin{align*}
       2\,\mat{C}\,\bm{v} - 2\,\lambda\,\bm{v} = \bm{0}
       \quad\Rightarrow\quad \mat{C}\,\bm{v} = \lambda\,\bm{v}
     \end{align*}
     (we used $\grad\,\bm{v}^\tr\mat{C}\,\bm{v} = 2\,\mat{C}\,\bm{v}$ for
     symmetric $\mat{C}$, from the mappings lecture)
   * So the stationary directions are the eigenvectors of $\mat{C}$,
     and the variance along eigenvector $\bm{v}_i$ is
     $\bm{v}_i^\tr\mat{C}\,\bm{v}_i = \lambda_i\,\bm{v}_i^\tr\bm{v}_i =
     \lambda_i$.  The direction of greatest variation is the eigenvector
     with the largest eigenvalue
   * The next best direction /orthogonal to the first/ is the eigenvector
     with the second largest eigenvalue, and so on

** Properties of the Covariance Matrix
   * $C_{ij} = \frac{1}{m-1}\sum_k (x_{ik}-\mu_i)(x_{jk}-\mu_j)$ measures
     how features $i$ and $j$ vary together, the sample version of
     $\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y) = \av{(X-\av{X})(Y-\av{Y})}$.  The diagonal holds
     the variances of the individual features
   * $\mat{C}$ is built from /outer products/ $\bm{a}\,\bm{a}^\tr$ (a
     $p\times p$ matrix), compared with the inner product
     $\bm{a}^\tr\bm{a}$ (a number)
   * $\mat{C}$ is symmetric, so its eigenvectors can be chosen
     orthonormal (eigensystems lecture)
   * $\mat{C}$ is /positive semi-definite/: for any $\bm{v}$,
     \begin{align*}
       \bm{v}^\tr\mat{C}\,\bm{v} = \bm{v}^\tr\mat{X}\,\mat{X}^\tr\bm{v}
       = \len{\mat{X}^\tr\bm{v}}^2 \geq 0
     \end{align*}
     Applying this to an eigenvector shows every eigenvalue is
     $\lambda_i = \bm{v}_i^\tr\mat{C}\,\bm{v}_i/\len{\bm{v}_i}^2 \geq 0$
     (they are variances, after all)
   * The total variance, summed over all features, is
     $\mathrm{Tr}\,\mat{C} = \sum_i C_{ii} = \sum_i\lambda_i$.  The
     fraction $\sum_{i\leq k}\lambda_i/\sum_i\lambda_i$ is the
     "variance explained" by the first $k$ components
   * *Zero eigenvalues.*  $\lambda_i=0$ exactly when there is no
     variation at all along $\bm{v}_i$
     * The $m$ centred points sum to $\bm{0}$, so they span at most an
       \((m-1)\)-dimensional subspace.  If $m\leq p$, $\mat{C}$ must have
       zero eigenvalues
     * With $m\geq p+1$ points that do not all lie in one hyperplane,
       every eigenvalue is positive: $\mat{C}$ is /positive definite/,
       full rank and invertible
     * Typically, with $m>p$, $\mat{C}$ is positive definite unless the
       features are linearly related (e.g. one is the sum of others)
   * *The ellipsoid picture.*  The surface
     $\bm{x}^\tr\mat{C}^{-1}\bm{x}=1$ is an ellipsoid whose principal axes
     point along the eigenvectors.  Along $\bm{v}_i$, putting
     $\bm{x}=r\,\bm{v}_i$ gives $r^2/\lambda_i=1$, so the radius is
     $\sqrt{\lambda_i}$: the standard deviation of the data in that
     direction

** Principal Component Analysis
   * The recipe
     1. Compute the mean $\bm{\mu}$ and the covariance matrix $\mat{C}$
     2. Find its eigenvalues and eigenvectors
     3. Keep the $k$ eigenvectors with the largest eigenvalues, the
        /principal components/
     4. Project each input into the space they span
   * The projection uses the $k\times p$ matrix whose rows are the
     principal components
     \begin{align*}
       \mat{P} = \begin{pmatrix} \bm{v}_1^\tr \\ \vdots \\ \bm{v}_k^\tr \end{pmatrix},
       \qquad
       \bm{z} = \mat{P}\,(\bm{x}-\bm{\mu})
     \end{align*}
     The $k$ numbers $z_j = \bm{v}_j^\tr(\bm{x}-\bm{\mu})$ are the new
     features
   * *Reconstruction.*  We can map back to an approximation of the input
     \begin{align*}
       \hat{\bm{x}} = \bm{\mu} + \sum_{j=1}^k z_j\,\bm{v}_j
     \end{align*}
   * *Reconstruction error.*  All $p$ eigenvectors form an orthonormal
     basis, so $\bm{x}-\bm{\mu} = \sum_{j=1}^p z_j\,\bm{v}_j$ exactly, and
     the error is the part we dropped,
     $\bm{x}-\hat{\bm{x}} = \sum_{j>k} z_j\,\bm{v}_j$.  By orthonormality
     $\len{\bm{x}-\hat{\bm{x}}}^2 = \sum_{j>k} z_j^2$, and averaging over
     the data (with the same $1/(m-1)$) each $z_j^2$ averages to the
     variance along $\bm{v}_j$, which is $\lambda_j$.  So
     \begin{align*}
       \frac{1}{m-1}\sum_{k'=1}^m \len{\bm{x}_{k'}-\hat{\bm{x}}_{k'}}^2
       = \sum_{j>k}\lambda_j
     \end{align*}
     the sum of the eigenvalues we ignored.  No other \(k\)-dimensional
     subspace does better, which is why PCA keeps the largest
     eigenvalues
   * *Choosing $k$.*  Plot the eigenvalues in decreasing order (as for the
     handwritten digits in the lecture) and keep enough components to
     explain most of the variance.  There is no single right answer
   * Images of handwritten digits: the leading eigenvectors look like
     blurred digits, and a few tens of them reconstruct a recognisable
     digit from its hundreds of pixels

** Duality
   * For images $p$ is huge: a $256\times256$ image has $p=65\,536$
     pixels, and $\mat{C}$ would have over $4\times10^9$ entries.  But $m$
     images span at most an \((m-1)\)-dimensional subspace, usually with
     $m\ll p$
   * Work instead with the $m\times m$ /dual matrix/
     $\mat{D} = \mat{X}^\tr\mat{X}$, whose entries
     $D_{kl} = \frac{1}{m-1}(\bm{x}_k-\bm{\mu})^\tr(\bm{x}_l-\bm{\mu})$
     are inner products between data points.  Building it costs
     $O(p\,m^2)$
   * If $\mat{D}\,\bm{v} = \lambda\,\bm{v}$, multiply on the left by
     $\mat{X}$:
     \begin{align*}
       \mat{X}\,\mat{X}^\tr\mat{X}\,\bm{v} = \lambda\,\mat{X}\,\bm{v}
       \quad\Rightarrow\quad \mat{C}\,\bm{u} = \lambda\,\bm{u},
       \qquad \bm{u} = \mat{X}\,\bm{v}
     \end{align*}
     so every eigenvector of $\mat{D}$ with $\lambda\neq0$ gives one of
     $\mat{C}$ with the same eigenvalue.  (If $\lambda=0$ then
     $\mat{X}\bm{v}=\bm{0}$ and there is nothing to map)
   * $\mat{C}$ and $\mat{D}$ therefore have the same /non-zero/
     eigenvalues.  The bigger matrix just has extra zeros
   * *Normalising.*  $\len{\mat{X}\,\bm{v}}^2 = \bm{v}^\tr\mat{D}\,\bm{v} =
     \lambda$ for a unit $\bm{v}$, so the unit eigenvector of $\mat{C}$
     is $\bm{u} = \mat{X}\,\bm{v}/\sqrt{\lambda}$
   * We have worked in the /dual space/, spanned by the examples.  The
     same trick reappears in SVMs and kernel methods, and the pair
     $\mat{X}\mat{X}^\tr$, $\mat{X}^\tr\mat{X}$ is the heart of the SVD
     (next lecture)

   1. Find the principal components and the variance along each
   2. What fraction of the variance does the first component explain?
   3. What are the radii and directions of the axes of the ellipse

   eigenvalue $\lambda>0$ then $\mat{X}\,\bm{v}/\sqrt{\lambda}$ is a unit

* Experiments

** PCA by hand
   * Generate data in 10 dimensions that really lies close to a plane,
     and check the claims in these notes

#+BEGIN_SRC python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
m, p = 200, 10
basis = rng.normal(size=(p, 2))  # data really lives near a plane
Y = (basis @ rng.normal(size=(2, m))).T + 0.1 * rng.normal(size=(m, p))
mu = Y.mean(axis=0)
X = (Y - mu).T / np.sqrt(m - 1)  # p x m, columns are centred points
C = X @ X.T  # covariance matrix (p x p)
lam, V = np.linalg.eigh(C)
lam, V = lam[::-1], V[:, ::-1]  # largest eigenvalue first
print(np.round(lam, 3))                           # two big, eight tiny
print(np.allclose(C, np.cov(Y.T)))  # agrees with numpy's covariance

for k in (1, 2, 3):
    P = V[:, :k].T  # k x p projection matrix
    Z = P @ (Y - mu).T                            # k x m new features
    Yhat = mu + (V[:, :k] @ Z).T                  # reconstruction
    err = np.sum((Y - Yhat) ** 2) / (m - 1)
    # the error should equal the sum of the discarded eigenvalues
    print(k, round(err, 4), round(lam[k:].sum(), 4))

# duality: with more features than examples use the m x m matrix
Xs = X[:, :5]  # pretend we have only 5 examples
print(np.round(np.linalg.eigvalsh(Xs.T @ Xs)[::-1], 4))  # 5 eigenvalues of D
print(np.round(np.linalg.eigvalsh(Xs @ Xs.T)[::-1], 4))  # same, plus zeros
#+END_SRC

   * Two eigenvalues (about 12 and 2.7) stand out; the other eight are
     around $0.01$, the noise.  Keeping $k=2$ components cuts the
     reconstruction error from about 2.8 to 0.08, and in every case the
     error equals the sum of the discarded eigenvalues
   * The $5\times5$ dual matrix has the same non-zero eigenvalues as the
     $10\times10$ matrix, which just has five extra zeros

* Answers

1. $\left(\bm{v}^\tr\bm{a}\right)^2 = (\bm{v}^\tr\bm{a})(\bm{a}^\tr\bm{v}) =
   \bm{v}^\tr\bm{a}\,\bm{a}^\tr\bm{v}$, since $\bm{v}^\tr\bm{a}$ is a
   number and equals its transpose.  With
   $\bm{a}=\bm{x}_k-\bm{\mu}$, summing over $k$ and taking $\bm{v}$
   outside the sum gives $\bm{v}^\tr\mat{C}\,\bm{v}$
2. The lecture example
   1. $\bm{\mu} = (5,5,3)^\tr$ and
      $\mat{X} = \begin{pmatrix} -3 & 3 \\ -1 & 1 \\ 2 & -2 \end{pmatrix}$
   2. $\mat{D} = \begin{pmatrix} 14 & -14 \\ -14 & 14 \end{pmatrix}$,
      with $\lambda=28$, $\bm{v} = (-1,1)^\tr/\sqrt{2}$ and $\lambda=0$,
      $\bm{v} = (1,1)^\tr/\sqrt{2}$
   3. $\mat{X}\,\bm{v} = (6,2,-4)^\tr/\sqrt{2}$ and dividing by
      $\sqrt{28}$ gives $\bm{u} = (3,1,-2)^\tr/\sqrt{14}$, with variance
      $28$.  The two centred points are $\pm(3,1,-2)^\tr$: they lie on a
      line through the origin, so there is no variation in the two
      directions perpendicular to it
3. $\mathrm{Tr}\,\mat{C} = \sum_i C_{ii}$ and $C_{ii}$ is the variance of
   feature $i$.  Using $\mat{C} = \mat{V}\mat{\Lambda}\mat{V}^\tr$ and the
   cyclic property of the trace,
   $\mathrm{Tr}\,\mat{C} = \mathrm{Tr}\,\mat{\Lambda}\mat{V}^\tr\mat{V}
   = \mathrm{Tr}\,\mat{\Lambda} = \sum_i\lambda_i$
4. The matrix from the eigensystems notes
   1. $\lambda=3$ along $(1,1)^\tr/\sqrt2$ and $\lambda=1$ along
      $(1,-1)^\tr/\sqrt2$.  The variances are $3$ and $1$
   2. $3/(3+1) = 75\%$
   3. Radii $\sqrt3$ along $(1,1)^\tr$ and $1$ along $(1,-1)^\tr$
5. $\mat{C}\,(\mat{X}\bm{v}) = \mat{X}\,(\mat{X}^\tr\mat{X}\,\bm{v}) =
   \lambda\,\mat{X}\bm{v}$, so it is an eigenvector, and
   $\len{\mat{X}\bm{v}}^2 = \bm{v}^\tr\mat{X}^\tr\mat{X}\,\bm{v} =
   \lambda\,\bm{v}^\tr\bm{v} = \lambda$, so dividing by $\sqrt{\lambda}$
   makes it a unit vector
