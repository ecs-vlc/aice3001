* Keywords
  * Gradient descent, Newton's method, linear, super-linear and quadratic convergence, condition number, BFGS, Levenberg--Marquardt

* Main Points

** Machine Learning as Optimisation
   * Once you've designed your learning machine and chosen your loss
     function the rest is optimisation: find the weights $\bm{w}$ that
     minimise the loss $L(\bm{w})$
   * This lecture covers the classical methods, which work well for up
     to thousands of parameters (L-BFGS: millions) when we can afford
     gradients computed from all the data.  The next lecture covers
     the very large problems of deep learning

** Gradient Descent
   * At a minimum (or a maximum, or a saddle point) $\grad L = \bm{0}$.
     For most models we cannot solve this, so we search iteratively
   * The gradient points in the direction in which the loss increases
     fastest, so we step the other way
     $$ \bm{w}^{(t+1)} = \bm{w}^{(t)} - r\,\grad L(\bm{w}^{(t)}) $$
   * The learning rate (step size) $r$ is the difficulty: too small and
     we crawl, too large and we diverge

** Newton's Method
   * Near a minimum almost every loss is quadratic (higher order terms
     in the Taylor expansion are negligible).  In one dimension, if
     $f(x) = a + \tfrac{b}{2}(x-x^*)^2$ then $f'(x) = b\,(x-x^*)$ and
     $f''(x) = b$, so
     $$ x^* = x - \frac{f'(x)}{f''(x)} $$
   * In many dimensions the second derivative becomes the Hessian,
     $H_{ij} = \partial^2 L/\partial w_i\,\partial w_j$, and
     $$ \bm{w}^* = \bm{w} - \mat{H}^{-1} \grad L(\bm{w}) $$
   * For a loss that is not exactly quadratic we iterate this.  Close to
     a minimum it converges /quadratically/: an error $\epsilon$ becomes
     one of order $\epsilon^2$, then $\epsilon^4$, and so on, roughly
     doubling the number of correct digits each step (Exercise 4)
   * Its drawbacks: the Hessian has $N^2$ entries and is costly to
     compute and invert, and far from a minimum Newton's method can head
     in the wrong direction (towards a maximum or saddle, or anywhere)

** Step Size and Different Length Scales
   * Near a minimum $\bm{w}^*$ take the quadratic approximation
     $L(\bm{w}) \approx L(\bm{w}^*) + \tfrac12(\bm{w}-\bm{w}^*)^\tr\mat{H}(\bm{w}-\bm{w}^*)$.
     A gradient step gives
     $$ \bm{w}^{(t+1)} - \bm{w}^* = (\mat{I} - r\,\mat{H})\,(\bm{w}^{(t)} - \bm{w}^*) $$
   * Write $\mat{H} = \mat{V}\mat{\Lambda}\mat{V}^\tr$ and
     $u_i = \bm{v}_i^\tr(\bm{w}-\bm{w}^*)$, the error along the \(i\)-th
     eigenvector.  Then each component evolves on its own:
     $$ u_i^{(t+1)} = (1 - r\,\lambda_i)\, u_i^{(t)} $$
   * Every component must shrink, $|1-r\lambda_i|<1$, so we need
     $r < 2/\lambda_{\max}$.  The steepest direction limits the step
     size
   * With $r$ that small, the flattest direction shrinks by only about
     $1 - \lambda_{\min}/\lambda_{\max}$ per step.  The best fixed step,
     $r = 2/(\lambda_{\max}+\lambda_{\min})$, gives the factor
     $$ \frac{\kappa-1}{\kappa+1}, \qquad \kappa = \frac{\lambda_{\max}}{\lambda_{\min}} $$
     where $\kappa$ is the condition number of the Hessian (eigensystems
     notes).  Badly conditioned problems ($\kappa\gg1$) are slow
   * *Rates of convergence.*  If the error shrinks by a constant factor
     each step, convergence is /linear/ (a straight line on a log
     plot).  If the factor itself tends to zero it is /super-linear/,
     and if the error is squared each step it is /quadratic/
     #+ATTR_LATEX: :width 0.75\textwidth
     [[file:figures/convRates.pdf]]
     Gradient descent is linear, quasi-Newton methods such as BFGS are
     super-linear, and Newton's method is quadratic

** Line Minimisation and Zig-Zags
   * Rather than a fixed step we can minimise along the gradient
     direction (a line search) at every step
   * At the line minimum the new gradient is orthogonal to the
     direction we came from, so in a narrow valley successive steps are
     at right angles: the path zig-zags across the valley rather than
     going along it

** Quasi-Newton Methods
   * These use curvature information without computing the full Hessian
   * *Conjugate gradient* chooses search directions that do not undo
     the progress made along earlier ones.  With exact line searches it
     reaches the minimum of an \(N\)-dimensional quadratic in $N$ steps
   * *BFGS* (Broyden--Fletcher--Goldfarb--Shanno) builds an
     approximation $\mat{B}\approx\mat{H}^{-1}$ from the gradients it has
     already seen, requiring at each step that
     $$ \mat{B}\,\bigl(\grad L(\bm{w}^{(t+1)}) - \grad L(\bm{w}^{(t)})\bigr) = \bm{w}^{(t+1)} - \bm{w}^{(t)} $$
     (for a quadratic, $\grad L$ changes by exactly $\mat{H}$ times the
     step).  It steps along $-\mat{B}\,\grad L$ with a line search and
     converges super-linearly
     * $\mat{B}$ is $N\times N$.  /L-BFGS/ keeps only the last ten or
       so steps and gradient changes, so its memory is $O(N)$
   * *Levenberg--Marquardt* is for least-squares losses
     $L(\bm{w}) = \len{\bm{\epsilon}(\bm{w})}^2$ with residuals
     $\epsilon_i = f(\bm{x}_i|\bm{w}) - y_i$
     * Linearise the residuals about the current weights,
       $\bm{\epsilon}(\bm{w}) \approx \bm{\epsilon}(\bm{w}^{(k)}) + \mat{J}(\bm{w}-\bm{w}^{(k)})$,
       where $\mat{J}$ is the Jacobian $J_{ij} = \partial\epsilon_i/\partial w_j$
     * Minimising the linearised loss gives the Gauss--Newton step
       $\bm{w}^{(k+1)} = \bm{w}^{(k)} - (\mat{J}^\tr\mat{J})^{-1}\mat{J}^\tr\bm{\epsilon}(\bm{w}^{(k)})$:
       Newton's method with $\mat{J}^\tr\mat{J}$ standing in for the
       Hessian
     * Adding a penalty $\nu\,\len{\bm{w}-\bm{w}^{(k)}}^2$ on the step
       size gives the Levenberg--Marquardt step (Exercise 5)
       $$ \bm{w}^{(k+1)} = \bm{w}^{(k)} - (\mat{J}^\tr\mat{J} + \nu\,\mat{I})^{-1}\mat{J}^\tr\bm{\epsilon}(\bm{w}^{(k)}) $$
     * Large $\nu$ gives a short step along the negative gradient; small
       $\nu$ gives Gauss--Newton.  $\nu$ is reduced when the linear model
       predicts the drop in loss well and increased when it does not
       (a /trust region/)
   * Non-gradient methods (Nelder--Mead, Powell's method, evolutionary
     strategies) also exist, but are not competitive in high dimensions

** Where These Methods Are Used
   * Logistic regression: scikit-learn's default solver is L-BFGS, and
     Newton's method is the standard fit for generalised linear models
   * Gaussian processes: the kernel hyper-parameters are usually fitted
     with L-BFGS
   * Curve fitting (non-linear least squares): Levenberg--Marquardt, e.g.
     SciPy's =curve_fit=
   * Support vector machines: training is a quadratic programme (later
     lectures)
   * With millions to billions of parameters, and gradients estimated
     from mini-batches, we need the methods of the next lecture

   3. Compute the eigenvalues of the Hessian

4. *Newton's method converges quadratically.*  Let $\epsilon = x - x^*$

   $\len{\bm{\epsilon} + \mat{J}\,\bm{d}}^2 + \nu\,\len{\bm{d}}^2$ over the

   What happens when $\nu$ is very large?

* Experiments

** Comparing optimisers on the Rosenbrock function
   * The Rosenbrock function $L = 100\,(w_2-w_1^2)^2 + (1-w_1)^2$ (the
     example in the lecture, with residuals $\epsilon_1 = 10\,(w_2-w_1^2)$
     and $\epsilon_2 = 1-w_1$) has a long, curved, narrow valley

#+BEGIN_SRC python
import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import minimize, least_squares, rosen, rosen_der, rosen_hess

x0 = np.array([-1.2, 1.0])        # the classic start; the minimum is at (1, 1)

x, r = x0.copy(), 1e-3            # gradient descent: a larger r diverges
for t in range(1, 200001):
    x = x - r * rosen_der(x)
    if np.linalg.norm(x - 1) < 1e-6: break
print("gradient descent:", t, "steps")

for method in ["BFGS", "L-BFGS-B", "trust-exact"]:   # trust-exact: Newton
    hess = rosen_hess if method == "trust-exact" else None
    res = minimize(rosen, x0, method=method, jac=rosen_der, hess=hess, tol=1e-12)
    print(f"{method}: {res.nit} iterations")

# Levenberg-Marquardt on the residuals from the lecture
eps = lambda x: np.array([10 * (x[1] - x[0]**2), 1 - x[0]])
res = least_squares(eps, x0, method="lm", xtol=1e-12)
print("Levenberg-Marquardt:", res.nfev, "function evaluations")
#+END_SRC

   * Gradient descent needs about 34,000 steps; BFGS, L-BFGS, Newton
     (with a trust region) and Levenberg--Marquardt need 20--40.  Try
     increasing =r= to see gradient descent diverge

* Answers

1. The update is $w^{(t+1)} = (1-r)\, w^{(t)}$
   1. The optimum step size is $r=1$, which jumps straight to the minimum at $w=0$
   2. $r_{\max}=2$, where $|1-r| = 1$
   3. $w^{(t)} = (1-r)^t \, w^{(0)}$: its size grows as $(r-1)^t$, with
      alternating sign
2. Quadratic optima
   1. $$ \grad L(\bm{w}) = \begin{pmatrix} w_1 - w_2 \\ 2\,w_2 - w_1 \end{pmatrix} $$
   2. $$ \mat{H} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1\\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix} $$
   3. Let $T = \mathrm{tr}\, \mat{H} =3$ and $D = \det(\mat{H}) = 1$ then
      $\lambda = \tfrac{1}{2} \left(T \pm \sqrt{T^2-4\,D}\right) =
      (3\pm\sqrt{5})/2 = \{0.382, 2.618\}$
   4. [[file:figures/quadProblem.pdf]]
3. Condition number
   1. $\kappa = 2.618/0.382 \approx 6.85$, and stability needs
      $r < 2/2.618 \approx 0.76$
   2. $r = 2/3$ gives the factor $(\kappa-1)/(\kappa+1) \approx 0.745$ per
      step, so we need $n$ with $0.745^n = 10^{-6}$:
      $n = \ln 10^{-6}/\ln 0.745 \approx 47$ steps
   3. One: the loss is exactly quadratic, so a single Newton step lands
      on the minimum
4. Expanding about $x^*$, where $f'(x^*)=0$:
   $f'(x) \approx f''\epsilon + \tfrac12 f'''\epsilon^2$ and
   $f''(x) \approx f'' + f'''\epsilon$ (derivatives at $x^*$).  Then
   \begin{align*}
     \epsilon' = \epsilon - \frac{f'(x)}{f''(x)}
     \approx \epsilon - \epsilon\,\frac{1 + \tfrac{f'''}{2f''}\epsilon}{1 + \tfrac{f'''}{f''}\epsilon}
     \approx \epsilon - \epsilon\left(1 - \frac{f'''}{2f''}\epsilon\right)
     = \frac{f'''}{2f''}\,\epsilon^2
   \end{align*}
   using $1/(1+a)\approx1-a$ for small $a$
5. The gradient with respect to $\bm{d}$ is
   $2\mat{J}^\tr(\bm{\epsilon}+\mat{J}\bm{d}) + 2\nu\,\bm{d}$.  Setting it to
   zero gives $(\mat{J}^\tr\mat{J}+\nu\,\mat{I})\,\bm{d} = -\mat{J}^\tr\bm{\epsilon}$.
   For large $\nu$, $\bm{d}\approx -\mat{J}^\tr\bm{\epsilon}/\nu =
   -\grad L/(2\nu)$: a short gradient-descent step
