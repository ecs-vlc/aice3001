* Main Points

** Singular Value Decomposition
   * Any $m\times p$ matrix, $\mat{X}$ can be decomposed as $\mat{X} = \mat{U}\,\mat{S}\,\mat{V}^\tr$
     (for a design matrix, $m$ is the number of data points and $p$ the
     number of features)
     * $\mat{U}$ is an $m\times m$ orthogonal matrix
     * $\mat{S}$ is an $m\times p$  matrix with zeros everywhere except the diagonal where $S_{ii}=s_i\geq0$
     * $\mat{V}$ is a $p\times p$ orthogonal matrix
   * The values $s_i$ are known as the /singular values/ of $\mat{X}$
   * The columns $\bm{u}_i$ of $\mat{U}$ and $\bm{v}_i$ of $\mat{V}$ are the
     left and right /singular vectors/.  Column by column the SVD says
     $\mat{X}\,\bm{v}_i = s_i\,\bm{u}_i$ and $\mat{X}^\tr\bm{u}_i = s_i\,\bm{v}_i$
   * If $\mat{X}$ is symmetric and positive semi-definite (e.g. a
     covariance matrix) then $\mat{U}=\mat{V}$ and $\mat{S}=\mat{\Lambda}$:
     the SVD is the eigen-decomposition.  For a general symmetric
     matrix $s_i = |\lambda_i|$, and the columns of $\mat{U}$ and $\mat{V}$
     belonging to a negative eigenvalue differ in sign
   * *Where it comes from.*  The SVD can be built from the eigenvectors
     of a symmetric matrix; the derivation is in the next section
   * *Economical SVD*
     * If $m>p$ some algorithms won't bother outputting the last $m-p$ columns of $\mat{U}$
     * If $p>m$ some algorithms won't bother outputting the last $p-m$ columns of $\mat{V}$
     * In this case it will output a square $\mat{S}$ matrix
     * In numpy, =U, s, Vt = np.linalg.svd(X)= gives the full form and
       =np.linalg.svd(X, full_matrices=False)= the economical one.  Note
       that numpy returns the singular values as a vector =s= and
       returns $\mat{V}^\tr$, not $\mat{V}$
   * *Finding the SVD.*  In principle we can take whichever of
     $\mat{X}\,\mat{X}^\tr$ and $\mat{X}^\tr\mat{X}$ is smaller, find its
     eigenvalues $s_i^2$ and eigenvectors, and recover the other set of
     singular vectors from $\mat{U} = \mat{X}\,\mat{V}\,\mat{S}^{-1}$ (for
     the non-zero $s_i$).  Good libraries do not do this: forming
     $\mat{X}^\tr\mat{X}$ squares the condition number (see the mappings
     notes), so they use a more stable algorithm on $\mat{X}$ itself

** Where the SVD Comes From
   * Take $m\leq p$ (otherwise apply the argument to $\mat{X}^\tr$) and
     build the $(m+p)\times(m+p)$ matrix
     \begin{align*}
       \mat{B} = \begin{pmatrix} \mat{0} & \mat{X} \\ \mat{X}^\tr & \mat{0} \end{pmatrix}
     \end{align*}
     It is symmetric, so it has real eigenvalues and orthonormal
     eigenvectors.  This is the whole trick: everything we know about
     symmetric matrices transfers to an arbitrary $\mat{X}$
   * *Eigenvectors come in pairs.*  Write an eigenvector as
     $(\bm{u},\bm{v})$, with $\bm{u}\in\mathbb{R}^m$ and
     $\bm{v}\in\mathbb{R}^p$.  Then $\mat{B}\,(\bm{u},\bm{v}) =
     s\,(\bm{u},\bm{v})$ says exactly
     \begin{align*}
       \mat{X}\,\bm{v} = s\,\bm{u}, \qquad \mat{X}^\tr\bm{u} = s\,\bm{v}
     \end{align*}
     Changing the sign of $\bm{v}$ changes the sign of both right-hand
     sides, so $(\bm{u},-\bm{v})$ is an eigenvector with eigenvalue $-s$.
     The eigenvalues therefore come as $m$ pairs $\pm s_i$ (with
     $s_i\geq0$) together with $p-m$ zeros, whose eigenvectors have the
     form $(\bm{0},\bm{v}_k)$ with $\mat{X}\,\bm{v}_k = \bm{0}$
   * Applying $\mat{X}^\tr$ to the first equation gives
     $\mat{X}^\tr\mat{X}\,\bm{v} = s\,\mat{X}^\tr\bm{u} = s^2\,\bm{v}$, and
     similarly $\mat{X}\,\mat{X}^\tr\bm{u} = s^2\,\bm{u}$: the $s_i^2$ are
     the eigenvalues of $\mat{X}^\tr\mat{X}$ and $\mat{X}\,\mat{X}^\tr$
   * *Normalisation.*  For $s_i>0$, $(\bm{u}_i,\bm{v}_i)$ and
     $(\bm{u}_i,-\bm{v}_i)$ have different eigenvalues, so they are
     orthogonal: $\len{\bm{u}_i}^2 - \len{\bm{v}_i}^2 = 0$.  Normalising the
     eigenvector gives $\len{\bm{u}_i}^2 + \len{\bm{v}_i}^2 = 1$, so
     $\len{\bm{u}_i}^2 = \len{\bm{v}_i}^2 = \tfrac12$.  In the same way,
     $(\bm{u}_i,\bm{v}_i)$ is orthogonal to both $(\bm{u}_j,\pm\bm{v}_j)$,
     giving $\bm{u}_i^\tr\bm{u}_j = \bm{v}_i^\tr\bm{v}_j = 0$ for $i\neq j$
     (for simplicity we take every $s_i>0$)
   * *Stacking the eigenvectors.*  Put the eigenvectors into the
     orthogonal matrix $\mat{W}$ and the eigenvalues into the diagonal
     matrix $\mat{D}$:
     \begin{align*}
       \mat{W} = \begin{pmatrix} \mat{U} & \mat{U} & \mat{0} \\
                                 \mat{V} & -\mat{V} & \mat{V}_0 \end{pmatrix},
       \qquad
       \mat{D} = \begin{pmatrix} \mat{S} & \mat{0} & \mat{0} \\
                                 \mat{0} & -\mat{S} & \mat{0} \\
                                 \mat{0} & \mat{0} & \mat{0} \end{pmatrix}
     \end{align*}
     where $\mat{U}$ ($m\times m$) and $\mat{V}$ ($p\times m$) have
     columns $\bm{u}_i$ and $\bm{v}_i$, $\mat{V}_0$ ($p\times(p-m)$) has
     columns $\bm{v}_k$, and $\mat{S} = \mathrm{diag}(s_1,\ldots,s_m)$.
     The eigen-decomposition of $\mat{B}$ is
     $\mat{B} = \mat{W}\,\mat{D}\,\mat{W}^\tr$
     #+ATTR_LATEX: :width 0.85\textwidth
     [[file:figures/svdMultiply-0.pdf]]
   * *Multiplying out.*  First multiply $\mat{W}$ by the diagonal matrix
     $\mat{D}$, which scales each block column.  Note that the $-\mat{V}$
     block meets $-\mat{S}$, giving $+\mat{V}\mat{S}$, and $\mat{V}_0$
     meets $\mat{0}$
     #+ATTR_LATEX: :width 0.85\textwidth
     [[file:figures/svdMultiply-1.pdf]]
   * Then multiply by $\mat{W}^\tr$.  The top-left block is
     $\mat{U}\mat{S}\mat{U}^\tr - \mat{U}\mat{S}\mat{U}^\tr = \mat{0}$, the
     top-right is $\mat{U}\mat{S}\mat{V}^\tr + \mat{U}\mat{S}\mat{V}^\tr =
     2\,\mat{U}\mat{S}\mat{V}^\tr$, and similarly for the bottom row
     #+ATTR_LATEX: :width 0.85\textwidth
     [[file:figures/svdMultiply-3.pdf]]
   * Comparing the two sides block by block,
     $\mat{X} = 2\,\mat{U}\,\mat{S}\,\mat{V}^\tr$.  The columns of
     $\mat{U}$ and $\mat{V}$ are orthogonal but have length
     $1/\sqrt{2}$, so define $\tilde{\mat{U}} = \sqrt{2}\,\mat{U}$ and
     $\tilde{\mat{V}} = \sqrt{2}\,\mat{V}$, whose columns are orthonormal.
     Then
     \begin{align*}
       \mat{X} = \tilde{\mat{U}}\,\mat{S}\,\tilde{\mat{V}}^\tr
     \end{align*}
     which is the (economical) SVD.  Adding the columns of $\mat{V}_0$ to
     $\tilde{\mat{V}}$ completes it to a $p\times p$ orthogonal matrix and
     gives the full SVD

** Spectrum and the Spectral Norm
   * The singular values, ordered $s_1\geq s_2\geq\cdots\geq0$, are the
     (singular value) /spectrum/ of $\mat{X}$
   * The largest, $s_{\max}$, is the most that $\mat{X}$ can stretch a
     vector.  It is the /spectral norm/ from the vector spaces lecture:
     \begin{align*}
       \len{\mat{X}}_2 = \max_{\bm{x}\neq\bm{0}}
       \frac{\len{\mat{X}\,\bm{x}}_2}{\len{\bm{x}}_2} = s_{\max}
     \end{align*}
   * *Proof.*  Write $\bm{x} = \sum_i c_i\,\bm{v}_i$ in the orthonormal
     basis of right singular vectors, so $\len{\bm{x}}^2 = \sum_i c_i^2$.
     Then $\mat{X}\,\bm{x} = \sum_i c_i\,s_i\,\bm{u}_i$, and as the
     $\bm{u}_i$ are orthonormal too
     \begin{align*}
       \len{\mat{X}\,\bm{x}}^2 = \sum_i s_i^2\,c_i^2
       \leq s_{\max}^2 \sum_i c_i^2 = s_{\max}^2\,\len{\bm{x}}^2
     \end{align*}
     with equality when $\bm{x} = \bm{v}_{\max}$, which is mapped to
     $\mat{X}\,\bm{v}_{\max} = s_{\max}\,\bm{u}_{\max}$
   * Consequences
     * For a symmetric matrix $\len{\mat{X}}_2 = \max_i|\lambda_i|$, as
       used in the eigensystems notes
     * For any square matrix $\len{\mat{X}}_2 \geq \max_i|\lambda_i|$
       (if $\mat{X}\bm{v}=\lambda\bm{v}$ then $\bm{v}$ is stretched by
       $|\lambda|$, which cannot exceed $s_{\max}$).  The two can differ
       a lot for non-symmetric matrices (Exercise 3)
     * $\len{\mat{X}}_F^2 = \sum_{ij}X_{ij}^2 = \sum_i s_i^2 \geq s_{\max}^2$,
       so $\len{\mat{X}}_2 \leq \len{\mat{X}}_F$ (Exercise 2)
     * For a square invertible matrix the smallest stretch is $s_{\min}$
       and $\len{\mat{X}^{-1}}_2 = 1/s_{\min}$, so the condition number is
       $\kappa = \len{\mat{X}}_2\,\len{\mat{X}^{-1}}_2 = s_{\max}/s_{\min}$.
       This generalises the eigenvalue ratio for symmetric matrices
     * Spectral normalisation of a network layer (vector spaces notes)
       divides $\mat{L}$ by $s_{\max}$ so that $\len{\mat{L}}_2 = 1$

** General Linear Mapping
   * Recall that matrices are the most general linear operators
   * Since any matrix $\mat{X}$ can be written as
     $\mat{U}\,\mat{S}\,\mat{V}^\tr$ we can interpret any linear
     mapping as doing three operations
     * A rotation (with possibly a reflection) defined by $\mat{V}^\tr$
     * A rescaling of each coordinate by $s_i$
     * A rotation (with possibly a reflection) defined by $\mat{U}$
   * When $\mat{X}$ is not square, $\mat{S}$ also either squashes some
     directions to zero or embeds the result in a space with extra
     dimensions
   * The /rank/ of any matrix is its number of non-zero singular values
     (the number of linearly independent rows or columns)
   * For a square matrix, using $|\mat{A}\mat{B}| = |\mat{A}|\,|\mat{B}|$
     and $|\mat{U}| = \pm1$ for an orthogonal matrix,
     $|\det\mat{X}| = \prod_i s_i$: the singular values multiply up to
     the change of volume
   * *Duality*
     * Using $\mat{X} = \mat{U}\,\mat{S}\,\mat{V}^\tr$ then
       * $\mat{C} = \mat{X}\,\mat{X}^\tr = \mat{U}\mat{S}\mat{S}^\tr\mat{U}^\tr$
       * $\mat{D} = \mat{X}^\tr\mat{X} = \mat{V}\mat{S}^\tr\mat{S}\mat{V}^\tr$
     * $\mat{S}\mat{S}^\tr$ and $\mat{S}^\tr\mat{S}$ are diagonal
       matrices whose non-zero diagonal elements are $s_i^2$

** Ridge Regression
   * Ridge regression is linear regression with an $L_2$ regulariser
   * Adding a regulariser $\eta\, \|\bm{w}\|^2$ to the squared loss, the weights, $\bm{w}^*$, that
     minimise the loss function are given by $\bm{w}^* =
     (\mat{X}^\tr\mat{X}+\eta\,\mat{I})^{-1}\mat{X}^\tr \bm{y}$
   * Using $\mat{X} = \mat{U}\,\mat{S}\,\mat{V}^\tr$ then
     \begin{align*}
     \bm{w}^* = \mat{V}\,\hat{\mat{S}}^+ \mat{U}^\tr \bm{y}
     \end{align*}
     where $\hat{\mat{S}}^+$ is a regularised pseudo-inverse of $\mat{S}$ given by
     \begin{align*}
     \hat{\mat{S}}^+ = (\mat{S}^\tr\mat{S}+\eta\,\mat{I})^{-1}\mat{S}^\tr
     \end{align*}
     - If $\eta=0$ (and all $s_i>0$) this is equal to the pseudo-inverse of $\mat{S}$
   * $\hat{\mat{S}}^+$ is a $p\times m$ matrix which is zero
     everywhere except on the diagonal, where $\hat{S}^+_{ii} = \frac{s_i}{s_i^2+\eta}$
     * Note if $s_i=0$ linear regression has an infinity of solutions
       and the pseudo-inverse of $\mat{X}$, as we derived it, does not
       exist (setting $\eta=0$ we get $S^+_{ii}=1/s_i$ which is not
       defined when $s_i=0$).  See the Moore--Penrose pseudo-inverse below
     * In the regularised case $\hat{S}^+_{ii} = 0$ (we have selected
       one of the solutions that minimise the squared error)
     * If $s_i\ll\eta$ then without the regularisation term the inverse is
       very ill-conditioned while with the regularisation term
       $\hat{S}^+_{ii}$ will be small
     * If $s_i\gg\eta$ then $\hat{S}^+_{ii} \approx \frac{1}{s_i} = S^+_{ii}$
   * Adding an $L_2$ regulariser means that the optimum weights,
     $\bm{w}^*$, will be less sensitive to the training data reducing
     the variance in the bias-variance dilemma

** The Moore--Penrose Pseudo-Inverse (going further)
   * The pseudo-inverse we derived,
     $\mat{X}^+ = (\mat{X}^\tr\mat{X})^{-1}\mat{X}^\tr$, only exists
     when $\mat{X}^\tr\mat{X}$ is invertible.  That needs every one of
     the $p$ singular values to be non-zero, which is impossible when
     $m<p$ (the under-constrained case)
   * Using the SVD it can be written
     $\mat{X}^+ = \mat{V}\,\mat{S}^+\mat{U}^\tr$, where $\mat{S}^+$ is
     the $p\times m$ diagonal matrix with $S^+_{ii} = 1/s_i$
   * The /Moore--Penrose pseudo-inverse/ makes one change: wherever
     $s_i=0$ it sets $S^+_{ii}=0$ instead of $1/s_i$
     \begin{align*}
       S^+_{ii} = \begin{cases} 1/s_i & s_i \neq 0 \\ 0 & s_i = 0 \end{cases}
     \end{align*}
     This always exists.  When $\mat{X}^\tr\mat{X}$ is invertible it is
     the same as our pseudo-inverse, and when $\mat{X}$ is square and
     invertible it is $\mat{X}^{-1}$
   * $\bm{w} = \mat{X}^+\bm{y}$ minimises $\len{\mat{X}\,\bm{w}-\bm{y}}^2$
     and, of all the weight vectors that do so, it is the one with the
     smallest $\len{\bm{w}}$
   * To see why, rotate to the coordinates given by the SVD: let
     $\bm{z} = \mat{V}^\tr\bm{w}$ and $\bm{c} = \mat{U}^\tr\bm{y}$.
     Orthogonal matrices do not change lengths, so
     $\len{\bm{w}} = \len{\bm{z}}$ and
     \begin{align*}
       \len{\mat{X}\,\bm{w}-\bm{y}}^2
       = \len{\mat{U}^\tr(\mat{U}\,\mat{S}\,\mat{V}^\tr\bm{w}-\bm{y})}^2
       = \len{\mat{S}\,\bm{z}-\bm{c}}^2
       = \sum_{i:\,s_i\neq0} (s_i\,z_i - c_i)^2 + \sum_{i:\,s_i=0} c_i^2
     \end{align*}
     (in the second sum $i$ runs over the rows of $\mat{S}$ that have no
     non-zero singular value)
     * The loss is minimised by $z_i = c_i/s_i$ whenever $s_i\neq0$
     * The other components of $\bm{z}$ do not appear in the loss at all:
       these are the directions $\bm{v}_i$ with $\mat{X}\,\bm{v}_i = \bm{0}$,
       where the data give no information
     * Every choice of them fits equally well.  Setting them to zero gives
       the smallest $\len{\bm{z}}=\len{\bm{w}}$, and that is exactly what
       $S^+_{ii}=0$ does
   * It is also the limit of ridge regression as the regularisation
     vanishes: as $\eta\to0$,
     $\hat{S}^+_{ii} = s_i/(s_i^2+\eta)$ tends to $1/s_i$ if $s_i\neq0$
     and is $0$ if $s_i=0$
   * This is what =np.linalg.pinv= computes, and =np.linalg.lstsq=
     returns the same minimum-norm solution.  In practice "zero" means
     below a small tolerance, so tiny singular values caused by rounding
     errors are discarded rather than inverted

* Experiments

** SVD
   * Check the claims in these notes on a random $3\times4$ matrix

#+BEGIN_SRC python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
X = rng.normal(size=(3, 4))
U, s, Vt = np.linalg.svd(X)       # s is a vector, Vt is V transposed
S = np.zeros_like(X)
np.fill_diagonal(S, s)            # the 3 x 4 matrix S
V = Vt.T
print(np.allclose(U @ S @ Vt, X))                        # X = U S V^T
print(np.allclose(U @ U.T, np.eye(3)), np.allclose(V.T @ V, np.eye(4)))
print(np.linalg.eigvalsh(X @ X.T)[::-1], s**2)           # eigenvalues are s_i^2
print(np.linalg.eigvalsh(X.T @ X)[::-1])                 # the same, plus a 0

# spectral norm: the largest stretch of any unit vector
print(np.linalg.norm(X, 2), s[0])                        # both s_max
print(np.linalg.norm(X @ V[:, 0]), np.linalg.norm(X @ V[:, 1]))  # s_1, s_2
x = rng.normal(size=4)
print(np.linalg.norm(X @ x) <= s[0] * np.linalg.norm(x))   # never above s_max
print(np.linalg.norm(X, 'fro')**2, np.sum(s**2))           # Frobenius^2

# ridge regression through the SVD
eta = 0.1
lhs = np.linalg.inv(X.T @ X + eta * np.eye(4))
rhs = V @ np.linalg.inv(S.T @ S + eta * np.eye(4)) @ V.T
print(np.allclose(lhs, rhs))
print(np.linalg.cond(X.T @ X))    # X^T X is singular (rank 3): enormous
#+END_SRC

   * Every check prints =True=.  The unit vector $\bm{v}_1$ is stretched
     by exactly $s_1$ and $\bm{v}_2$ by $s_2$
   * $\mat{X}^\tr\mat{X}$ is $4\times4$ but only has rank 3, so it has no
     inverse.  Rounding errors mean numpy may still return one, with a
     condition number around $10^{16}$: a warning sign never to ignore.
     Adding $\eta\,\mat{I}$ makes it safely invertible

* Answers
** Ridge regression
   1. It is straightforward to show
      $$ \bm{w}^* = (\mat{X}^\tr\,\mat{X}+\eta\,\mat{I})^{-1}
      \mat{X}^\tr \bm{y} $$
   2. The only hard part is to show that
      \begin{align*}
       (\mat{X}^\tr\mat{X} + \eta\,\mat{I})^{-1} 
       = \mat{V}\,(\mat{S}^\tr\mat{S}+\eta\,\mat{I})^{-1}\mat{V}^\tr
       \end{align*}
      * It is easy to show that $\mat{X}^\tr\mat{X}=\mat{V}\,\mat{S}^\tr\mat{S}\,\mat{V}^\tr$
      * But we also have $\mat{I}=\mat{V}\,\mat{V}^\tr$ as $\mat{V}$ is an orthogonal matrix
      * Thus $\mat{M} = \mat{X}^\tr\mat{X} + \eta\,\mat{I} =
        \mat{V}\,(\mat{S}^\tr\mat{S}+\eta\mat{I})\mat{V}^\tr =
        \mat{V}\,\mat{W}\,\mat{V}^\tr$ where $\mat{W}= \mat{S}^\tr\mat{S}+\eta\mat{I}$
      * But $(\mat{A}\,\mat{B}\,\mat{C})^{-1} =
        \mat{C}^{-1}\mat{B}^{-1}\mat{A}^{-1}$ (which we can verify by
        multiplying $\mat{C}^{-1}\mat{B}^{-1}\mat{A}^{-1}$ on either
        the left or right by $\mat{A}\,\mat{B}\,\mat{C}$)
      * Thus $\mat{M}^{-1} = (\mat{V}\,\mat{W}\,\mat{V}^\tr)^{-1} =
        (\mat{V}^\tr)^{-1}\,\mat{W}^{-1}\mat{V}^{-1} =
        \mat{V}\,\mat{W}^{-1}\mat{V}^\tr$ where we use
        $\mat{V}^{-1}=\mat{V}^\tr$ as $\mat{V}$ is an orthogonal matrix
** Spectral norm
   1. See the proof in the main points: expand $\bm{x}$ in the right
      singular vectors, so $\len{\mat{X}\bm{x}}^2 = \sum_i s_i^2c_i^2
      \leq s_{\max}^2\sum_i c_i^2$.  For $\bm{x}=\bm{v}_{\max}$ only one
      $c_i$ is non-zero and the inequality is an equality
   2. $\sum_{ij}X_{ij}^2 = \sum_j (\mat{X}^\tr\mat{X})_{jj} =
      \mathrm{Tr}\,\mat{X}^\tr\mat{X}$.  With
      $\mat{X}^\tr\mat{X} = \mat{V}\,\mat{S}^\tr\mat{S}\,\mat{V}^\tr$ and the
      cyclic property of the trace this is $\mathrm{Tr}\,\mat{S}^\tr\mat{S}
      = \sum_i s_i^2$.  Every term is non-negative, so the sum is at least
      $s_{\max}^2$
   3. Two $2\times2$ matrices
      1. $\mat{J}$ is triangular, so its eigenvalues are its diagonal
         entries, $1$ and $1$
      2. $\mat{J}^\tr\mat{J} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$
         has $\lambda^2 - 3\lambda + 1 = 0$, so $\lambda = (3\pm\sqrt{5})/2$
         and the singular values are $s = (\sqrt{5}\pm1)/2$, i.e. $1.618$
         and $0.618$.  So $\len{\mat{J}}_2 = (1+\sqrt5)/2 \approx 1.618$
         (the golden ratio) although every eigenvalue is $1$, and
         $\kappa = 1.618/0.618 \approx 2.62$.  The eigenvalues of a
         non-symmetric matrix do not tell you how much it stretches
      3. Here $s_i = |\lambda_i| = 3$ and $1$, so the spectral norm is $3$
         and $\kappa = 3$.  For a symmetric matrix the eigenvalues do tell
         the whole story
