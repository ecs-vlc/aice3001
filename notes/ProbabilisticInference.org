* Main Points

** Beyond Conjugate Priors
   * In the lecture on Bayesian inference we chose priors so that the
     posterior had the same form as the prior (conjugate priors)
   * Real problems involve many interacting random variables, some of
     which we never observe, and the posterior then has no simple
     closed form
   * This lecture is about how to set up such models and about simple
     strategies for using them: MAP, empirical Bayes and the EM
     algorithm.  The lecture on MCMC shows how to compute the full
     posterior
   * Everything rests on the two rules from the lecture on probability
     - the sum rule (marginalisation):
       $\Prob{X} = \sum_y \Prob{X,Y=y}$, or $f(x) = \int f(x,y)\,\dd y$
     - the product rule (the definition of conditional probability):
       $\Prob{X,Y} = \Prob{X|Y}\,\Prob{Y} = \Prob{Y|X}\,\Prob{X}$, of
       which Bayes' rule is a rearrangement
     - conditioning does not imply causation

** Building Probabilistic Models
   * *Discriminative Models*
     - Often in machine learning our goal is to learn the probability
       distribution $\Prob{Y|\bm{X}}$ where $Y$ is our target and
       $\bm{X}$ is a data point
     - We may parameterise this distribution with some
       parameters $\bm{\Theta}$ and our task would be to learn these
       parameters based on training data
   * *Generative Models*
     - Surprisingly it is  often easier to model the joint probability
       $\Prob{Y,\bm{X}}$
     - This means that we model the process of both generating the
       targets and the feature vectors together
     - These are known as /generative models/ as they allow us to
       generate random examples
     - We don't necessarily want to use them to generate random samples;
       it just makes the modelling process easier (although you need
       to get used to this as it feels counter-intuitive)
     - We can use generative models to do discrimination since
       $\Prob{Y|\bm{X}} = \Prob{Y,\bm{X}}/\Prob{\bm{X}}$ where
       $\Prob{\bm{X}}=\sum_{Y} \Prob{Y,\bm{X}}$ (the sum is over the
       few values of the target, not over all possible feature vectors)
   * *Latent Variables*
     - To model complicated processes we introduce intermediate random
       variables that we never observe
     - These are known as *latent variables*; they are not our target,
       but they make the model easy to write down
     - To predict we marginalise them out:
       $\Prob{\bm{X},Y} = \sum_Z \Prob{\bm{X},Y,Z}$
   * *Generative Stories*
     - A good way to build a model is to write down how the data could
       have been generated, as if writing a simulator.  Each line draws
       one variable given variables drawn earlier.  For the ratings
       model below
#+BEGIN_SRC python
theta = rng.beta(a, b, size=J)    # a rate for each product
k = rng.binomial(n, theta)        # observed positive reviews
#+END_SRC
     - By the product rule the joint distribution is the product of the
       distributions in each line
       $$ f(\bm{\theta},\bm{k}) = \prod_{j=1}^J
          \mathrm{Beta}(\theta_j|a,b)\,\mathrm{Bin}(k_j|n_j,\theta_j) $$
     - /Probabilistic programming languages/ such as Stan and PyMC let
       you write a model in this form and then do the inference
       (usually by MCMC) for you
   * *Graphical Models*
     - The same information can be drawn as a diagram (Figure
       \ref{fig:gmratings}): each random variable is a node, and arrows
       point to a variable from the variables it is drawn from
     - We use dark nodes for fixed numbers (parameters or known
       quantities), shaded nodes for observed variables and white
       nodes for latent variables
     - A box (a /plate/) means "repeat for each": here for each product
       $j$
     - The joint distribution is the product, over nodes, of the
       distribution of each node given its parents
     - Graphical models are also used to read off which variables are
       independent, but we only use them to design and explain models

** Hierarchical Models
   * A shop lists products with the fraction of reviews that are
     positive.  Product $A$ has 3 positive reviews out of 3 (100%),
     product $B$ 1840 out of 2000 (92%).  Which is better?
   * Ranking by the fraction puts every product with a handful of good
     reviews at the top.  Intuitively we trust $A$'s 100% less, and our
     experience that most products are rated about 80% should pull it
     down
   * *Model.*  Product $j$ has an unknown probability $\theta_j$ that a
     review is positive, and $k_j$ of its $n_j$ reviews are positive
     $$ k_j \sim \mathrm{Bin}(n_j,\theta_j), \qquad
        \theta_j \sim \mathrm{Beta}(a,b) $$
     - the products are different, but similar: their $\theta_j$ come
       from a common population
     - $a$ and $b$ are /hyperparameters/: parameters of the
       distribution of the parameters.  $a/(a+b)$ is the typical rate
       and $a+b$ measures how alike the products are (the variance of
       $\mathrm{Beta}(a,b)$ is $ab/((a+b)^2(a+b+1))$, which is small
       when $a+b$ is large)
     - the model has levels (population, product, reviews), so it is
       called a /hierarchical model/
   * *One product at a time.*  If we knew $a$ and $b$, each product
     would be the beta-binomial problem of the lecture on Bayesian
     inference, with posterior
     $$ f(\theta_j|k_j) = \mathrm{Beta}(\theta_j|a+k_j,\,b+n_j-k_j) $$
     The posterior mean is a weighted average of the typical rate and
     the observed fraction
     $$ \av{\theta_j|k_j} = \frac{a+k_j}{a+b+n_j}
        = \frac{a+b}{a+b+n_j}\,\frac{a}{a+b} +
          \frac{n_j}{a+b+n_j}\,\frac{k_j}{n_j} $$
     so the prior counts as $a+b$ extra reviews.  With $a=20$ and
     $b=5$ (typical rate 0.8, worth 25 reviews) product $A$ gets
     $23/28\approx0.82$ and product $B$ gets $1860/2025\approx0.92$:
     $B$ is better
   * *Shrinkage.*  Products with few reviews are pulled (/shrunk/)
     towards the typical rate, while those with many reviews barely
     move (Figure \ref{fig:shrink}).  Each product /borrows strength/
     from the others
      #+ATTR_LATEX: :width 0.7\textwidth
      #+CAPTION: Fraction of positive reviews (red) and posterior means (blue) for 30 products; $A$ and $B$ are the examples above.  The hyperparameters were fitted by empirical Bayes\label{fig:shrink}
	[[./figures/ratingsShrink-1.pdf]]
   * *Partial pooling.*  There are two extremes: estimate each product
     on its own ($k_j/n_j$: no pooling), which is noisy when $n_j$ is
     small, or treat all products as the same (complete pooling), which
     ignores real differences.  A hierarchical model sits in between,
     and the data decide where, through $a+b$.  The same idea is used
     for schools, hospitals, adverts, sports players and the users of a
     recommender system
   * But we don't know $a$ and $b$.  If we give them a prior too (a
     /hyperprior/), the posterior $f(a,b,\bm{\theta}|\bm{k})$ is no longer
     conjugate and has no closed form: $a$ and $b$ appear inside the
     beta function that normalises $\mathrm{Beta}(\theta_j|a,b)$

** Solution Strategies
   * *Full Bayes.*  Bayes' rule gives the posterior over all the
     parameters
     $$ \Prob{\bm{\Theta}|\mathcal{D}} =
        \frac{\Prob{\mathcal{D}|\bm{\Theta}}\,\Prob{\bm{\Theta}}}{\Prob{\mathcal{D}}} $$
     - it describes our uncertainty and, because it averages over
       parameters rather than choosing the best, it is much less prone
       to over-fitting
     - but it is difficult: the posterior is rarely a standard
       distribution, and the /evidence/ or /marginal likelihood/
       $$ \Prob{\mathcal{D}} = \sum_{\bm{\Theta}} \Prob{\mathcal{D}|\bm{\Theta}} \, \Prob{\bm{\Theta}}
       \qquad\text{or}\qquad
       f(\mathcal{D}) = \int  f(\mathcal{D}|\bm{\theta}) \, f(\bm{\theta})\,\dd \bm{\theta} $$
       is a sum or integral over far too many values when
       $\bm{\Theta}$ is high dimensional
     - Monte Carlo methods (the lecture on MCMC) can sample from the
       posterior without computing the evidence, but they can be slow
   * *Maximum a posteriori (MAP).*  Take the mode of the posterior
     $$ \bm{\theta}_{\text{\small MAP}} = \argmax_{\bm{\theta}}
       f(\mathcal{D}|\bm{\theta})\, f(\bm{\theta})  =
       \argmax_{\bm{\theta}} \logg{f(\mathcal{D}|\bm{\theta})} +
       \logg{f(\bm{\theta})} $$
     - we don't need the evidence, which does not depend on $\bm{\theta}$
     - $-\logg{f(\bm{\theta})}$ acts as a regulariser.  For example a
       Gaussian prior $w_i\sim\normal{w_i}{0,s^2}$ on weights adds
       $\|\bm{w}\|^2/(2s^2)$ to the loss: ridge regression, or weight
       decay in neural networks.  We use this in the lecture on matrix
       factorisation
     - but it isn't really Bayesian (although some users will claim it
       is).  It keeps a single point and throws away our uncertainty
       about $\bm{\theta}$, so we lose our error bars and can over-fit
     - the peak of a probability density is not invariant: change
       variables (e.g. from $\sigma$ to $\log\sigma$) and the peak
       moves, although the probabilities do not
     - in high dimensions most of the posterior probability can lie
       far from the peak, so the MAP point can be untypical
   * *Maximum likelihood.*  With a uniform prior MAP is the /maximum
     likelihood estimate/
     - this was developed and popularised by Ronald Fisher
       (1912--1922) at a time when Bayesian inference was considered
       taboo, and was accepted by the statistical community despite its
       strong connection to Bayesian inference
     - in high-dimensional problems a non-uniform prior can make a
       big difference
   * *Empirical Bayes.*  For a hierarchical model a half-way house is
     to integrate out the many parameters $\theta_j$, choose the few
     hyperparameters to maximise the evidence (as we did for the
     hyperparameters of a Gaussian process), and then use the
     conjugate posterior for each product
     - for the ratings model the integral is in closed form, the
       /beta-binomial/ distribution
       $$ \Prob{k_j|a,b} = \int_0^1 \binom{n_j}{k_j}\theta^{k_j}(1-\theta)^{n_j-k_j}
          \frac{\theta^{a-1}(1-\theta)^{b-1}}{B(a,b)}\,\dd\theta
          = \binom{n_j}{k_j}\frac{B(a+k_j,\,b+n_j-k_j)}{B(a,b)} $$
       where $B(a,b) = \int_0^1\theta^{a-1}(1-\theta)^{b-1}\dd\theta =
       \Gamma(a)\Gamma(b)/\Gamma(a+b)$ is the beta function
     - we maximise $\sum_j\log\Prob{k_j|a,b}$ numerically; for the 30
       products of Figure \ref{fig:shrink} this gives $a=19.9$ and
       $b=5.3$
     - it is cheap and often works well, but it ignores our
       uncertainty in $a$ and $b$
   * *A ladder of strategies*
     | Strategy           | What you get                           | Cost                 |
     |--------------------+----------------------------------------+----------------------|
     | Maximum likelihood | a point estimate                       | an optimiser         |
     | MAP                | a regularised point estimate           | an optimiser         |
     | Empirical Bayes    | posteriors given fitted hyperparameters | an integral          |
     | EM                 | ML or MAP with latent variables        | iteration            |
     | Full Bayes         | all the uncertainty                    | MCMC                 |
     Choose the cheapest strategy that answers your question

** You Already Know EM
   * \(k\)-means clustering repeats two steps: assign each point to its
     nearest centre, then move each centre to the mean of its points
   * The first step guesses the hidden cluster labels; the second fits
     the parameters as if those guesses were right
   * The EM algorithm does the same, but with /probabilities/ of
     belonging to each cluster, and it comes from a probabilistic
     model, a mixture

** Mixtures of Gaussians
    * To illustrate latent variables and a simple hierarchical model
      we consider a classic probabilistic model known as a /mixture of Gaussians/
    * We consider a concrete scenario
    * We suppose we are detecting two types ($A$ and $B$) of
      short-lived particles [fn:1]
    * We measure the mass, $X_i$, of each particle with a noisy
      detector, but we don't know the type of particle
    * Let $Z_i \in \{0,1\}$ equal 1 if  particle $i$ is of type $A$
      and 0 if it is of type $B$, with $\Prob{Z_i=1}=p$
    * The probability density of the measurement is
      therefore
      $$ f(X_i|Z_i,\bm{\Theta}) = Z_i\,\normal{X_i}{\mu_A,\sigma_A^2} +
        (1-Z_i)\,\normal{X_i}{\mu_B,\sigma_B^2} $$
      * where $\mu_A$ and $\mu_B$ are the masses of particles of
        type $A$ and $B$ respectively
      * $\sigma_A$ and $\sigma_B$ are the standard deviations in the measurements
      * this says that if the $i^{th}$ particle is of type $A$
        then the density of $X_i$ is
        $\normal{X_i}{\mu_A,\sigma_A^2}$, while if it is of type $B$,
        then $X_i$ is distributed according to $\normal{X_i}{\mu_B,\sigma_B^2}$
    * We show some typical data  from $m=1\,000$ observations in
      Figure \ref{fig:data}
      #+ATTR_LATEX: :width 0.6\textwidth
      #+CAPTION: Example of data measuring the masses of two types of particles\label{fig:data}
	[[./figures/mog-0.pdf]]
    * Our job is to infer the parameters $\bm{\Theta}=(\mu_A,
      \sigma_A^2, \mu_B, \sigma_B^2, p)$
    * As a graphical model (Figure \ref{fig:gmmix}): for each data point
      draw a type $Z_i$, then a mass $X_i$ from that type's normal
      distribution.  $Z_i$ is latent; the parameters are shared by all
      the data points, so they sit outside the plate
      #+ATTR_LATEX: :width 0.5\textwidth
      #+CAPTION: Graphical model for a mixture of two Gaussians\label{fig:gmmix}
	[[./figures/gmModels-1.pdf]]
    * We could do a full Bayesian calculation, but let us just use
      maximum likelihood
    * Assuming the data points are independent, the likelihood of the data
      $\mathcal{D}=\{X_i|i=1,2,\ldots,m\}$ is
       \begin{align*}
       f(\mathcal{D}|\bm{\Theta}) 
       &\eq \sum_{\bm{Z}\in\{0,1\}^m}  f(\mathcal{D},\bm{Z}|\bm{\Theta}) \\
       &\eq \prod_{i=1}^m \sum_{Z_i\in\{0,1\}} f(X_i, Z_i | \bm{\Theta})
       \eq \prod_{i=1}^m  \sum_{Z_i\in\{0,1\}}
       f(X_i | Z_i, \bm{\Theta}) \, \Prob{Z_i|\bm{\Theta}}
       \end{align*}
       \explanation
      1) where we marginalise out the latent variables
         $\bm{Z}=(Z_1,Z_2,\ldots Z_m)$
      2) we assume the data points are independent, so the sum over
         $\bm{Z}$ factorises into a product of sums over each $Z_i$
      3) we use the identity $f(X_i, Z_i | \bm{\Theta})= f(X_i | Z_i, \bm{\Theta}) \, \Prob{Z_i|\bm{\Theta}}$
    * It is usually easier working with the log-likelihood
      \begin{align*}
      \logg{f(\mathcal{D}|\bm{\Theta})} &= \sum_{i=1}^m
      \logg{\strut f(X_i|Z_i=1) \, \Prob{Z_i=1} + f(X_i|Z_i=0) \,
      \Prob{Z_i=0} }\\
       &=\sum_{i=1}^m
      \logg{p\,\normal{X_i}{\mu_A,\sigma_A^2}+(1-p)\,\normal{X_i}{\mu_B,\sigma_B^2}}
      \end{align*}
    * We could do gradient ascent on this, but it is an ugly
      expression to work with: the log of a sum does not simplify
    * Strictly, the likelihood has no maximum: put $\mu_A = X_1$ and let
      $\sigma_A\to0$.  Then $f(X_1|\bm{\Theta}) \geq
      p\,\normal{X_1}{X_1,\sigma_A^2} = p/(\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma_A)\to\infty$,
      while every other data point still has a density of at least
      $(1-p)\,\normal{X_i}{\mu_B,\sigma_B^2}>0$.  What we want is a good
      /local/ maximum

** Expectation Maximisation
    - Rather than maximise the likelihood directly we can start from
      some initial guess $\bm{\Theta}^{(0)}$ and iteratively improve it
      \begin{equation}
      \bm{\Theta}^{(t+1)} = \argmax_{\bm{\Theta}} Q(\bm{\Theta}|\bm{\Theta}^{(t)}),
      \qquad
      Q(\bm{\Theta}|\bm{\Theta}^{(t)}) =
      \sum_{\bm{Z}\in\{0,1\}^m} \Prob{\bm{Z}\middle|\mathcal{D},\bm{\Theta}^{(t)}}\,
      \logg{f(\mathcal{D},\bm{Z} | \bm{\Theta})} \label{eq:em}
      \end{equation}
      + $\logg{f(\mathcal{D},\bm{Z} | \bm{\Theta})}$ is the
        log-likelihood we would have if we knew the latent variables
        (the /complete-data/ log-likelihood); it is usually simple
      + we don't know $\bm{Z}$, so we average over it using its
        distribution given the data and our current parameters
    - This is a general optimisation strategy that is regularly used
      when we have latent variables
    - It is known as *expectation maximisation* or the *EM-algorithm*
      + E-step: compute $\Prob{\bm{Z}\middle|\mathcal{D},\bm{\Theta}^{(t)}}$
      + M-step: maximise $Q(\bm{\Theta}|\bm{\Theta}^{(t)})$
    - This looks very different to maximising the log-likelihood: it
      takes some effort to understand why this works
    - By the definition of conditional probability
      $$ f(\mathcal{D},\bm{Z}|\bm{\Theta}) =
      \Prob{\bm{Z}|\mathcal{D},\bm{\Theta}}\, f(\mathcal{D}|\bm{\Theta}) $$
      from which (taking logs and rearranging), for every $\bm{Z}$,
      $$ \logg{f(\mathcal{D}|\bm{\Theta})} =
      \logg{f(\mathcal{D},\bm{Z}|\bm{\Theta})} -
      \logg{\Prob{\bm{Z}|\mathcal{D},\bm{\Theta}}} $$
    - Multiply both sides by $\Prob{\bm{Z}\middle|\mathcal{D},\bm{\Theta}^{(t)}}$
      and sum over $\bm{Z}$.  The left-hand side does not involve
      $\bm{Z}$, and the probabilities sum to 1, so
      $$ \logg{f(\mathcal{D}|\bm{\Theta})} =
      Q(\bm{\Theta}|\bm{\Theta}^{(t)}) + S(\bm{\Theta}|\bm{\Theta}^{(t)}) $$
      where $Q$ is the function we maximise in equation (\ref{eq:em}) and
      $$ S(\bm{\Theta}|\bm{\Theta}^{(t)}) = -
         \sum_{\bm{Z}\in\{0,1\}^m} \Prob{\bm{Z}\middle|\mathcal{D},\bm{\Theta}^{(t)}}\,
        \logg{\Prob{\bm{Z}|\mathcal{D},\bm{\Theta}}} $$
    - The change in log-likelihood when we update our parameters is
      therefore
      \begin{align*}
      \Delta L &=
      \logg{f(\mathcal{D}|\bm{\Theta}^{(t+1)})} -
      \logg{f(\mathcal{D}|\bm{\Theta}^{(t)})} \\
      &= Q(\bm{\Theta}^{(t+1)}|\bm{\Theta}^{(t)}) -
      Q(\bm{\Theta}^{(t)}|\bm{\Theta}^{(t)})
      + S(\bm{\Theta}^{(t+1)}|\bm{\Theta}^{(t)}) -
      S(\bm{\Theta}^{(t)}|\bm{\Theta}^{(t)})
       \end{align*}
    - The difference of the $S$ terms is
      $$ \sum_{\bm{Z}} P_t(\bm{Z}) \logg{\frac{P_t(\bm{Z})}{P_{t+1}(\bm{Z})}}
      = \mathrm{KL}\!\left( P_t \middle\| P_{t+1} \right) $$
      where $P_t(\bm{Z}) = \Prob{\bm{Z}|\mathcal{D},\bm{\Theta}^{(t)}}$; this
      is the /KL divergence/, which we meet again in the lecture on entropy
    - KL divergences are never negative.  Using $\log(x)\leq x-1$,
      $$ -\mathrm{KL}\!\left( P_t \middle\| P_{t+1} \right) =
      \sum_{\bm{Z}} P_t(\bm{Z}) \logg{\frac{P_{t+1}(\bm{Z})}{P_t(\bm{Z})}}
      \leq \sum_{\bm{Z}} P_t(\bm{Z}) \left(\frac{P_{t+1}(\bm{Z})}{P_t(\bm{Z})} -1\right)
      = 1 - 1 = 0 $$
    - In expectation maximisation we choose $\bm{\Theta}^{(t+1)}$ to
      maximise $Q(\bm{\Theta}|\bm{\Theta}^{(t)})$, so
      $Q(\bm{\Theta}^{(t+1)}|\bm{\Theta}^{(t)}) \geq
      Q(\bm{\Theta}^{(t)}|\bm{\Theta}^{(t)})$
    - Thus $\Delta L\geq 0$: no step decreases the log-likelihood.  (We
      only need $Q$ to increase, so a partial M-step also works.)
    - To find a MAP solution instead, add $\logg{f(\bm{\Theta})}$ to $Q$
    - Let us return to our two types of particles $A$ and $B$
    - The complete-data likelihood is $f(\mathcal{D},\bm{Z} |\bm{\Theta}) =
      \prod\limits_{i=1}^m  f(X_i,Z_i|\bm{\Theta})$ where
      $$ f(X_i,Z_i|\bm{\Theta}) = p\, Z_i\,\normal{X_i}{\mu_A,\sigma_A^2} +
        (1-p)\,(1-Z_i)\,\normal{X_i}{\mu_B,\sigma_B^2} $$
    - E-step.  Given the data, the $Z_i$ are independent and by Bayes' rule
      \begin{align*}
      \gamma_i^{(t)} = \Prob{Z_i=1\middle|X_i, \bm{\Theta}^{(t)}} &= 
      \frac{p^{(t)}\, \normal{X_i}{\mu_A^{(t)},\sigma_A^{2(t)}} }
     { p^{(t)}\,\normal{X_i}{\mu_A^{(t)},\sigma_A^{2(t)}} + (1-p^{(t)})\, \normal{X_i}{\mu_B^{(t)},\sigma_B^{2(t)}} }
      \end{align*}
      and $\Prob{Z_i=0\middle|X_i, \bm{\Theta}^{(t)}} = 1-\gamma_i^{(t)}$.
      $\gamma_i^{(t)}$ is called the /responsibility/ of type $A$ for
      data point $i$
    - M-step.  Because $\logg{f(\mathcal{D},\bm{Z}|\bm{\Theta})}$ is a
      sum over data points, the sum over $\bm{Z}$ in $Q$ reduces to a
      sum over each $Z_i$
      \begin{align*}
      Q(\bm{\Theta}|\bm{\Theta}^{(t)}) &= \sum_{i=1}^m \;
      \gamma_i^{(t)} \logg{p\,\normal{X_i}{\mu_A,\sigma_A^{2}}}
      + (1-\gamma_i^{(t)}) \logg{(1-p)\,\normal{X_i}{\mu_B,\sigma_B^{2}}} \\ 
      &= \sum_{i=1}^m \;
      \gamma_i^{(t)}\left(\log(p) -
      \frac{(X_i-\mu_A)^2}{2\sigma_A^{2}}
	- \frac{1}{2} \logg{2\,\pi\,\sigma_A^{2}} \right) \\
	&\hspace{1cm}
	+ (1-\gamma_i^{(t)})\left(\log(1-p) -
	\frac{(X_i-\mu_B)^2}{2\sigma_B^{2}}
	- \frac{1}{2} \logg{2\,\pi\,\sigma_B^{2}} \right) 
	\end{align*}
    - To maximise this we just set the derivatives to 0
      + With respect to $p$
         $$ \frac{\partial Q(\bm{\Theta}|\bm{\Theta}^{(t)})}{\partial p}
          = \frac{1}{p} \sum_{i=1}^m \gamma_i^{(t)} - \frac{1}{1-p}
          \sum_{i=1}^m (1-\gamma_i^{(t)}) =0 
	  \quad\Rightarrow\quad
	  p^{(t+1)} = \frac{1}{m} \sum\limits_{i=1}^m  \gamma_i^{(t)}$$
      + With respect to $\mu_A$
        $$ \frac{\partial Q(\bm{\Theta}|\bm{\Theta}^{(t)})}{\partial \mu_A} 
	= \sum\limits_{i=1}^m \gamma_i^{(t)} \frac{X_i-\mu_A}{\sigma_A^{2}} = 0$$
	so (and similarly for $\mu_B$)
	$$ \mu_A^{(t+1)} = \frac{ \sum\limits_{i=1}^m
        \gamma_i^{(t)} X_i }{\sum\limits_{i=1}^m \gamma_i^{(t)}} ,\quad\quad
	\mu_B^{(t+1)} = \frac{ \sum\limits_{i=1}^m
        (1-\gamma_i^{(t)}) X_i }{\sum\limits_{i=1}^m (1-\gamma_i^{(t)})} $$
      + With respect to $\sigma_A^2$
         $$ \frac{\partial Q(\bm{\Theta}|\bm{\Theta}^{(t)})}{\partial \sigma^2_A}
	 = \frac{1}{2\,\sigma^4_A}\sum_{i=1}^m \gamma_i^{(t)}
         (X_i-\mu_A)^2- \frac{1}{2\,\sigma_A^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^m \gamma_i^{(t)} = 0$$
	so, putting in the optimal $\mu_A^{(t+1)}$ (and similarly for $\sigma^2_B$)
	$$ \sigma_A^{2(t+1)} = \frac{ \sum\limits_{i=1}^m
        \gamma_i^{(t)} (X_i-\mu^{(t+1)}_A)^2 }{\sum\limits_{i=1}^m \gamma_i^{(t)}}
        ,\quad\quad
	 \sigma_B^{2(t+1)} = \frac{ \sum\limits_{i=1}^m
        (1-\gamma_i^{(t)}) (X_i-\mu^{(t+1)}_B)^2 }{\sum\limits_{i=1}^m (1-\gamma_i^{(t)})}$$
    - These are very natural update equations
      + we estimate the probability, $\gamma_i^{(t)}$, that observation $X_i$
        is a particle of type $A$ based on our current parameters
      + we then compute the fraction, means and variances of each type,
        weighting each data point by these probabilities
      + if the $\gamma_i^{(t)}$ were all 0 or 1 these would be the
        ordinary fraction, means and variances of each group
    - We are guaranteed that our EM-algorithm never decreases the
      likelihood (although it could reach a local rather than global optimum)
    - For the data set we showed earlier (which was a random sample
      of size 1000 generated using $p=0.3$, $\mu_A=4$,
      $\sigma_A=0.8$, $\mu_B=8$ and $\sigma_B=2$) we get the results
      shown in Figure \ref{fig:mog}

      #+CAPTION: Example of EM algorithm to compute the statistics for the masses of our two particles \label{fig:mog}
	[[./figures/mog-1.pdf]]
    - The EM algorithm often leads to very natural update equations,
      but its convergence is often rather slow

** EM and \(k\)-Means
   * Give both clusters the same fixed variance $\sigma^2$.  Then
     $$ \gamma_i = \frac{1}{1 + \frac{1-p}{p}\,
        \e{\left((X_i-\mu_A)^2 - (X_i-\mu_B)^2\right)/(2\sigma^2)}} $$
     and as $\sigma\to0$ (with $0<p<1$ fixed) $\gamma_i\to1$ if $X_i$ is
     nearer $\mu_A$ and $\gamma_i\to0$ if it is nearer $\mu_B$
   * The E-step becomes "assign each point to its nearest mean" and the
     M-step "move each mean to the average of its points": \(k\)-means is
     EM with hard assignments
   * EM's soft assignments let a point in the overlap count for both
     clusters, and EM also learns the size ($p$) and width ($\sigma$) of
     each cluster

** EM in Practice
   * EM finds a local maximum that depends on the starting point, so
     run it from several starting points and keep the best
   * A component can collapse onto a single point ($\sigma\to0$), where
     the likelihood is infinite.  A prior on $\sigma$ (i.e. MAP, which
     just adds $\logg{f(\bm{\Theta})}$ to $Q$) prevents this
   * Convergence can be slow when the components overlap, because the
     latent variables are then very uncertain
   * The update equations are simple and need no step size
   * The same idea applies to any model with latent variables: missing
     data, hidden Markov models, and many more

** Lower Bounds and VAEs
   * (This section goes beyond the lecture: it is a pointer to where
     these ideas lead.)
   * The proof that EM works can be written for /any/ distribution
     $q(\bm{Z})$ over the latent variables.  Since
     $f(\mathcal{D}|\bm{\Theta}) = f(\mathcal{D},\bm{Z}|\bm{\Theta}) /
     \Prob{\bm{Z}|\mathcal{D},\bm{\Theta}}$ for every $\bm{Z}$,
     \begin{align*}
     \logg{f(\mathcal{D}|\bm{\Theta})}
     &= \sum_{\bm{Z}} q(\bm{Z}) \logg{\frac{f(\mathcal{D},\bm{Z}|\bm{\Theta})}
        {\Prob{\bm{Z}|\mathcal{D},\bm{\Theta}}}} \\
     &= \sum_{\bm{Z}} q(\bm{Z}) \logg{\frac{f(\mathcal{D},\bm{Z}|\bm{\Theta})}{q(\bm{Z})}}
      + \sum_{\bm{Z}} q(\bm{Z}) \logg{\frac{q(\bm{Z})}{\Prob{\bm{Z}|\mathcal{D},\bm{\Theta}}}}
     \end{align*}
     The second term is $\mathrm{KL}\bigl(q\,\|\,\Prob{\bm{Z}|\mathcal{D},\bm{\Theta}}\bigr)\geq0$,
     so the first term is a lower bound on the log-likelihood, called
     the /evidence lower bound/ (ELBO)
   * EM is coordinate ascent on the ELBO: the E-step sets $q$ to the
     posterior $\Prob{\bm{Z}|\mathcal{D},\bm{\Theta}^{(t)}}$, which makes
     the KL term zero so that the bound touches the log-likelihood;
     the M-step maximises the bound over $\bm{\Theta}$ (the
     $-\sum q\log q$ part does not depend on $\bm{\Theta}$, so this is
     maximising $Q$)
   * A /variational autoencoder/ (VAE) is a latent-variable model in
     which a neural network (the decoder) generates data from a latent
     vector.  Its posterior over the latent vector is intractable, so a
     second network (the encoder) learns an approximation $q$, and both
     are trained by gradient ascent on the same bound
   * We meet the KL divergence again in the lecture on entropy

#+BEGIN_SRC python
import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import minimize
from scipy.special import betaln

rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
J = 200
n = np.round(np.exp(rng.uniform(np.log(2), np.log(2000), J))).astype(int)
theta = rng.beta(16, 4, J)                   # true a = 16, b = 4
k = rng.binomial(n, theta)


def neg_log_evidence(log_ab):                # binomial coefficients omitted:
    a, b = np.exp(log_ab)                    # they do not depend on a, b
    return -np.sum(betaln(a + k, b + n - k) - betaln(a, b))


a, b = np.exp(minimize(neg_log_evidence, np.log([1.0, 1.0]),
                       method="Nelder-Mead").x)
post = (a + k) / (a + b + n)
print("a, b:", a, b)
small = n < 10
for name, est in [("k/n", k / n), ("posterior mean", post)]:
    print(name, "RMS error, all:", np.sqrt(np.mean((est - theta) ** 2)),
          " n<10:", np.sqrt(np.mean((est - theta)[small] ** 2)))
#+END_SRC

** EM for a mixture of Gaussians

     log-likelihood as we go
#+BEGIN_SRC python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
m = 1000
p, muA, sA, muB, sB = 0.3, 4.0, 0.8, 8.0, 2.0      # true parameters
Z = rng.random(m) < p                                # latent types
X = np.where(Z, rng.normal(muA, sA, m), rng.normal(muB, sB, m))


def normal(x, mu, var):
    return np.exp(-(x - mu) ** 2 / (2 * var)) / np.sqrt(2 * np.pi * var)


th = dict(p=0.5, muA=2.0, vA=1.0, muB=10.0, vB=1.0)   # initial guess
for t in range(51):
    a = th["p"] * normal(X, th["muA"], th["vA"])
    b = (1 - th["p"]) * normal(X, th["muB"], th["vB"])
    loglik = np.sum(np.log(a + b))
    if t in (0, 1, 2, 5, 10, 20, 50):
        print(f"t={t:2d}  log-lik {loglik:9.2f}  p={th['p']:.3f}  "
              f"muA={th['muA']:.2f}  sA={np.sqrt(th['vA']):.2f}  "
              f"muB={th['muB']:.2f}  sB={np.sqrt(th['vB']):.2f}")
    g = a / (a + b)                                    # E-step: responsibilities
    th["p"] = g.mean()                                 # M-step
    th["muA"] = np.sum(g * X) / np.sum(g)
    th["muB"] = np.sum((1 - g) * X) / np.sum(1 - g)
    th["vA"] = np.sum(g * (X - th["muA"]) ** 2) / np.sum(g)
    th["vB"] = np.sum((1 - g) * (X - th["muB"]) ** 2) / np.sum(1 - g)
#+END_SRC
   + Does the log-likelihood ever decrease?  How quickly do the
     parameters settle down?  Try other starting points.  (See the answers.)

** Posterior means for ratings
   - By fraction $C$ (0.90) beats $D$ (0.85).  By posterior mean
     $C$ gets $29/35\approx0.83$ and $D$ gets $105/125=0.84$, so $D$
     ranks higher: $C$'s ten reviews are not enough to be sure it is
     better than a typical product
   - $\frac{a+b}{a+b+n}\cdot\frac{a}{a+b} + \frac{n}{a+b+n}\cdot\frac{k}{n}
     = \frac{a}{a+b+n} + \frac{k}{a+b+n} = \frac{a+k}{a+b+n}$

** The beta-binomial
   - Collecting the powers of $\theta$,
     \begin{align*}
     \Prob{k|a,b} &= \int_0^1 \binom{n}{k}\theta^{k}(1-\theta)^{n-k}\,
        \frac{\theta^{a-1}(1-\theta)^{b-1}}{B(a,b)}\,\dd\theta
     = \frac{\binom{n}{k}}{B(a,b)}\int_0^1\theta^{a+k-1}(1-\theta)^{b+n-k-1}\dd\theta \\
     &= \binom{n}{k}\frac{B(a+k,\,b+n-k)}{B(a,b)}
     \end{align*}
   - $\binom{n}{k}$ does not depend on $a$ or $b$, so it only adds a
     constant to the log-evidence and does not change where its maximum is

** Empirical Bayes for ratings
   - The fit gives $a=19.6$ and $b=4.8$ (the true values are 16 and 4;
     the typical rates, $0.80$ and $0.80$, agree well)
   - The root-mean-square error of the estimates of $\theta_j$ falls
     from $0.116$ for the raw fractions to $0.050$ for the posterior
     means.  For the products with fewer than 10 reviews it falls from
     $0.236$ to $0.073$: shrinkage helps most where the data are few

** Generative to discriminative
   - By Bayes' rule
     $$ \Prob{Z=1|X} = \frac{0.3\,\normal{5.5}{4,0.64}}
     {0.3\,\normal{5.5}{4,0.64} + 0.7\,\normal{5.5}{8,4}}
     = \frac{0.3\times0.0860}{0.3\times0.0860 + 0.7\times0.0913}
     \approx 0.29 $$
   - Although $5.5$ is closer to $\mu_A$ than to $\mu_B$, type $B$ is
     both more common and much more spread out, so it is the more
     likely explanation

** One EM step by hand
   - $\normal{x}{\mu,1}\propto\e{-(x-\mu)^2/2}$ and $p=\frac12$, so
     $\gamma_i = 1/(1+\e{-((X_i-\mu_B)^2-(X_i-\mu_A)^2)/2})$.  For
     $X=0,1,5,6$ the exponents $(X_i-\mu_B)^2-(X_i-\mu_A)^2$ are
     $24, 16, -16, -24$, so
     $\gamma \approx (1.000, 1.000, 0.000, 0.000)$ (more precisely
     $1-6\times10^{-6}$, $1-3.4\times10^{-4}$, $3.4\times10^{-4}$, $6\times10^{-6}$)
   - $p = \frac14\sum\gamma_i = 0.5$, $\mu_A \approx (0+1)/2 = 0.50$ and
     $\mu_B \approx (5+6)/2 = 5.50$: the clusters are so well separated
     that EM reduces to splitting the data into two groups and
     averaging each

** Mysterious Disease
   - We want to compute $f(p,r_0|\mathcal{D})$ where
     $\mathcal{D}=(X(1), X(2), \ldots, X(T))$
   - We have a likelihood of 
     $$ \Prob{X(t)\middle|Z(t),p} = \mathrm{Binom}(X(t)|Z(t), p) $$
     where
     $$ \Prob{Z(t+1)|r_0,Z(t),Z(t-1),Z(t-2)} = \mathrm{Poi}\!\left(Z(t+1)\middle|
     \frac{r_0}{3}\, (Z(t)+Z(t-1)+Z(t-2)) \right) $$
   - To perform a Bayesian calculation we would have to put priors,
     $f(p)$ and $f(r_0)$, on $p$ and $r_0$
     + A reasonable prior to use for $p$ is \(f(p) =
       \mathrm{Beta}(p|a,b)\)---you could use $a=b=1$ as an uninformative
       (uniform) prior, but you might have some prior knowledge, e.g. $a=b=2$ 
       which says $f(p)=6\,p\,(1-p)$
     + A reasonable prior for $r_0$ would be
       \(\mathrm{Gamma}(r_0|a,b)\)---you could use $a=b=0$ as an
       uninformative (improper) prior but again you might have some
       prior belief, e.g. $a=2$, $b=1$
   - Bayes' rule tells us
     $$ f(p,r_0,\bm{Z}|\bm{X}) = \frac{\prod\limits_{t=1}^T
     \Prob{X(t)\middle|Z(t),p}\,
     \Prob{Z(t)|Z(t-1),Z(t-2),Z(t-3),r_0} \,
     f(r_0)\,f(p)}{\Prob{\bm{X}}} $$
   - To get estimates of $p$ and $r_0$ we have to marginalise out $\bm{Z}$
   - Now the problem is this is rather horrible to compute (your
     priors are not conjugate priors in this problem and the posterior
     is very complicated)
   - Probably the best way to do this is to use Markov Chain Monte
     Carlo (MCMC), which is the subject of a later lecture
   - The simulator above gives, for $r_0=2$ and $p=0.2$, case numbers
     that grow by roughly a factor of 1.45 a day; the reported numbers
     $X(t)$ are about a fifth of $Z(t)$, but noisy while they are small
