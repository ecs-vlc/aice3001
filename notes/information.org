* Main Points

** Communicating Down a Noisy Channel
   * Information theory studies sending a message $X$, drawn with
     probability $\Prob{X}$, down a noisy channel that delivers $Y$ with
     probability $\Prob{Y\mid X}$
   * Having received $Y = y$, our remaining uncertainty about what was sent is
     $$ H_{X\mid Y=y} = -\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\Prob{X=x\mid Y=y}\,\log\!\bra{\Prob{X=x\mid Y=y}} $$
   * Averaging over what we might receive gives the /conditional entropy/
     $$ H_{X\mid Y} = \sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\Prob{Y=y}\,H_{X\mid Y=y} = -\sum_{x,y}\Prob{X=x,Y=y}\,\log\!\bra{\Prob{X=x\mid Y=y}} $$

** Joint Entropy
   * The /joint entropy/ is the entropy of the pair,
     $$ H_{X,Y} = -\sum_{x,y}\Prob{X=x,Y=y}\,\log\!\bra{\Prob{X=x,Y=y}} $$
   * If $X$ and $Y$ are independent then $H_{X,Y} = H_X + H_Y$ (see the
     previous lecture); if they are correlated, in general
     $H_{X,Y}\neq H_X + H_Y$
   * Taking logs of $\Prob{X,Y} = \Prob{Y\mid X}\,\Prob{X} = \Prob{X\mid Y}\,\Prob{Y}$
     and averaging gives the /chain rule/ for entropy
     $$ H_{X,Y} = H_X + H_{Y\mid X} = H_Y + H_{X\mid Y} $$
     and hence $H_X - H_{X\mid Y} = H_Y - H_{Y\mid X}$

** Mutual Information
   * Before receiving anything our uncertainty about $X$ is $H_X$.
     Shannon defined the /mutual information/ as the expected reduction in
     that uncertainty when we receive the message:
     $$ I_{X;Y} = H_X - H_{X\mid Y} $$
   * By the chain rule result above, $I_{X;Y} = I_{Y;X}$: $Y$ tells us as
     much about $X$ as $X$ tells us about $Y$
   * The relationships between these quantities are summarised in the
     diagram below
     #+ATTR_LATEX: :width 0.55\linewidth
     [[file:figures/mutualinf.pdf]]
   * The /capacity/ of a channel is the largest information gain we can
     achieve by choosing the distribution of messages,
     $$ C = \max_{\Prob{X}} I_{X;Y} $$
   * Mutual information is widely used in machine learning; for example,
     two sets of images can be aligned by choosing the non-linear
     transformation that maximises the (normalised) mutual information
     between them

** The Binary Symmetric Channel
   * The simplest noisy channel sends bits, each flipped independently with
     probability $f$:
     $$ \Prob{Y=1\mid X=0} = \Prob{Y=0\mid X=1} = f $$
   * With equally likely inputs, an elementary calculation gives, per bit,
     $$ H_{X_i\mid Y_i} = -(1-f)\log_2(1-f) - f\log_2(f) = H(f) $$
     and for a message of $n$ bits $H_{X\mid Y} = n\,H(f)$
     #+ATTR_LATEX: :width 0.35\linewidth
     [[file:figures/oneBitEntropy.pdf]]
   * Since $H_X = n$ bits for uniform inputs, $I_{X;Y} = n\,(1 - H(f))$.
     Uniform inputs are optimal here, so the capacity is $1 - H(f)$ bits
     per bit sent

** Error Correcting Codes and Shannon's Bound
   * To communicate reliably we use an error correcting code: choose code
     words far apart in the space of binary strings, so that a corrupted
     code word is still closer to the original than to any other
     #+ATTR_LATEX: :width 0.4\linewidth
     [[file:figures/shannonBound.pdf]]
   * A /Hamming ball/ $B(\bm{x}, r)$ is the set of strings that differ from
     $\bm{x}$ in at most $r$ positions
   * A string of length $n$ suffers about $n\,f$ errors, and for large $n$
     almost never more than $(f+\epsilon)\,n$.  So if each code word
     owns a ball of radius $r = (f+\epsilon)\,n$, and the balls don't
     overlap, errors can be corrected
   * By Stirling's approximation a ball of radius $f\,n$ contains
     $\binom{n}{fn} \approx 2^{n H(f)}$ strings, so at most
     $$ \frac{2^n}{2^{nH(f)}} = 2^{n(1 - H(f))} $$
     non-overlapping balls fit.  We can therefore send at most
     $n\,(1 - H(f))$ bits of information in $n$ bits: the rate cannot
     exceed the capacity
   * Shannon also showed that choosing $2^{nI_{X;Y}}$ code words at random
     keeps them far enough apart with high probability, so this rate is
     /achievable/
   * *Example.*  For $f = 0.1$, $H(0.1) \approx 0.469$ bits, so the capacity
     is $C = 1 - 0.469 \approx 0.531$ bits.  Reliable communication needs
     codes about $1/0.531\approx1.9$ times as long as the message---just
     under twice as long---for a channel that corrupts 10% of bits
   * Random codes cannot be decoded efficiently, so Shannon's result told
     us the bound was achievable without telling us how.  Practical codes
     that come close to it (such as turbo and LDPC codes) took decades more
     to find

** KL-Divergence
   * The /Kullback--Leibler divergence/ is
     $$ \mathrm{KL}\bra{p\,\|\,q} = \av[X\sim p]{\log\!\bra{\frac{p(X)}{q(X)}}} = \av[X\sim p]{-\log\!\bra{q(X)}} - H_p $$
   * $-\log(q(x))$ is the code length for $x$ in the optimal code for the
     distribution $q$.  So
     $$ \av[X\sim p]{-\log(q(X))} $$
     is the expected message length when data from $p$ is coded as if it
     came from $q$, and
     $\mathrm{KL}\bra{p\,\|\,q}$ is the expected /extra/ length paid for using
     the wrong distribution
   * It is also called the /relative entropy/.  It is never negative, is
     zero only when $p = q$, and is not symmetric:
     $\mathrm{KL}\bra{p\,\|\,q}\neq\mathrm{KL}\bra{q\,\|\,p}$ in general

** Variational Approximation
   * In Bayesian inference the posterior $f(\bm{\theta}\mid\mathcal{D})$ is
     often too complicated to write down, which is why we used MCMC
   * The /variational approximation/ instead fits a simpler distribution,
     typically factorised,
     $$ f(\bm{\theta}\mid\mathcal{D}) \approx g(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{\phi}) = \prod_i g(\theta_i\mid\phi_i) $$
     by maximising the /variational free energy/
     $$ \Phi(\bm{\phi}) = -\int g(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{\phi})\,\log\!\bra{\frac{g(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{\phi})}{f(\bm{\theta},\mathcal{D})}}\,\dd\bm{\theta} $$

** The Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO)
   * Writing $f(\bm{\theta},\mathcal{D}) = f(\bm{\theta}\mid\mathcal{D})\,f(\mathcal{D})$,
     \begin{align*}
       \Phi(\bm{\phi})
         &= -\int g(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{\phi})\bra{\log\!\bra{\frac{g(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{\phi})}{f(\bm{\theta}\mid\mathcal{D})}} - \log\!\bra{f(\mathcal{D})}}\dd\bm{\theta} \\
         &= \log\!\bra{f(\mathcal{D})} - \mathrm{KL}\bra{g(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{\phi})\,\|\,f(\bm{\theta}\mid\mathcal{D})}
     \end{align*}
   * Because the KL-divergence is non-negative,
     $\Phi(\bm{\phi})\leq\log\!\bra{f(\mathcal{D})}$: the free energy is a
     lower bound on the log evidence, hence the name ELBO
   * Maximising $\Phi$ minimises the KL-divergence, making $g$ close to the
     true posterior and $\Phi\approx\log\!\bra{f(\mathcal{D})}$
   * Equivalently $\Phi(\bm{\phi}) = L_g(\bm{\phi}) + H_g(\bm{\phi})$ with
     \begin{align*}
       L_g(\bm{\phi}) &= \int g(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{\phi})\,\log\!\bra{f(\mathcal{D}\mid\bm{\theta})\,f(\bm{\theta})}\,\dd\bm{\theta}
       & H_g(\bm{\phi}) &= -\int g(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{\phi})\,\log\!\bra{g(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{\phi})}\,\dd\bm{\theta}
     \end{align*}
     The first term rewards putting $g$ where the joint density is large
     (compare MAP); the entropy term rewards keeping $g$ as spread out as
     possible
   * Variational methods are usually much faster than MCMC and can be very
     accurate, but the quality is not guaranteed.  MCMC is a controlled
     approximation that improves with more iterations; its slowness is
     rarely a problem on modern computers
   * Minimising the divergence the other way round,
     $\mathrm{KL}\bra{f\,\|\,g}$, leads to a different family of methods
     (expectation propagation)

** Minimum Description Length
   * Outside the Bayesian framework, choosing between models of different
     complexity is hard.  A validation set is the usual answer, but is not
     always possible
   * A principled alternative: prefer the model that /compresses the data
     most/.  If a model compresses the data it has captured real structure
   * *Alice and Bob.*  Alice has data
     $\mathcal{D} = \{(\bm{x}_i,y_i)\mid i=1,\ldots,m\}$; Bob has only the
     inputs $\bm{x}_i$.  Both have a model $\hat{f}(\bm{x}\mid\bm{\theta})$.
     Rather than sending all the $y_i$, Alice sends the parameters
     $\bm{\theta}$ and the errors $\delta_i = y_i - \hat{f}(\bm{x}_i\mid\bm{\theta})$
   * If the errors have density $p_\delta$, sending one error to accuracy
     $\Delta$ costs $-\log\!\bra{p_\delta(\delta_i)\,\Delta}$.  The
     /description length/ is
     $$ L = \sum_{k=1}^n \ell(\theta_k) - \sum_{i=1}^m\bra{\log\!\bra{p_\delta\!\bra{y_i - \hat{f}(\bm{x}_i\mid\bm{\theta})}} + \log(\Delta)} $$
     where $\ell(\theta_k)$ is the cost of sending parameter $\theta_k$, to
     whatever precision is worthwhile
   * The /minimum description length/ (MDL) principle chooses the model with
     the smallest $L$.  The $-m\log(\Delta)$ term is the same for every model
     and does not affect the choice
   * For Gaussian errors $-\log(p_\delta(\delta))$ is the squared error plus
     a constant, so MDL is least squares plus a penalty for model complexity
   * MDL trades accuracy against complexity and is often the only
     principled method available.  It can be fiddly because we must decide
     how precisely to store the parameters, though good models often
     survive surprisingly low precision

** Variational Auto-Encoders
   * A variational auto-encoder (VAE) encodes an input $\bm{x}$ into a
     distribution $q_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{z}\mid\bm{x})$ over a latent code
     $\bm{z}$ and decodes $\bm{z}$ back to a reconstruction.  Its loss is
     $$ \mathcal{L} = \av[\bm{x}\sim\mathcal{D}]{\mathrm{KL}\bra{q_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{z}\mid\bm{x})\,\|\,\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\mat{I})} - \log\!\bra{p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}\mid\bm{z}(\bm{x}))}} $$
     #+ATTR_LATEX: :width 0.8\linewidth
     [[file:figures/vae-5.pdf]]
   * The original paper derived this as a variational approximation to a
     posterior, which is hard to follow.  It has a much more natural reading
     as a description length:
     - Alice encodes each image into a latent code
       $q_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{z}\mid\bm{x})$ and sends it, at a cost of
       $$ \mathrm{KL}\bra{q_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{z}\mid\bm{x})\,\|\,\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\mat{I})} $$
     - she also sends the errors needed to repair Bob's reconstruction,
       at a cost of
       $$ -\log\!\bra{p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}\mid\bm{z}(\bm{x}))} $$
   * Minimising the loss minimises the total description length.  The
     clever part is that the network also chooses how precisely to send
     the code, trading code cost against repair cost

* Exercises

** The binary symmetric channel
   * Verify that with equally likely inputs $H_{X_i\mid Y_i} = H(f)$, and
     hence that $I_{X_i;Y_i} = 1 - H(f)$ bits.  Evaluate the capacity for
     $f = 0.1$ and $f = 0.5$, and explain the second answer

** Mutual information from entropies
   * Use the chain rule for entropy to show that
     $I_{X;Y} = H_X + H_Y - H_{X,Y}$

** The KL-divergence is not symmetric
   * For two coins with heads probabilities $p = 0.5$ and $q = 0.9$,
     compute $\mathrm{KL}\bra{p\,\|\,q}$ and $\mathrm{KL}\bra{q\,\|\,p}$ in bits

** KL-divergence between normals (harder)
   * Show that
     $$ \mathrm{KL}\bra{\mathcal{N}(\mu_1,\sigma_1^2)\,\|\,\mathcal{N}(\mu_2,\sigma_2^2)} = \log\!\bra{\frac{\sigma_2}{\sigma_1}} + \frac{\sigma_1^2 + (\mu_1-\mu_2)^2}{2\,\sigma_2^2} - \frac{1}{2} $$
     in nats

* Answers

** The binary symmetric channel
   * With uniform inputs and symmetric noise, Bayes' rule gives
     $\Prob{X=y\mid Y=y} = 1-f$ and $\Prob{X\neq y\mid Y=y} = f$ whichever
     $y$ is received, so $H_{X_i\mid Y=y} = H(f)$ for both $y$, and the
     average is $H(f)$
   * $H_{X_i} = 1$ bit, so $I_{X_i;Y_i} = 1 - H(f)$
   * $f = 0.1$: $C \approx 1 - 0.469 = 0.531$ bits.  $f = 0.5$: $H(0.5) = 1$,
     so $C = 0$.  When each bit is flipped with probability one half the
     output is independent of the input, and nothing gets through

** Mutual information from entropies
   * From $H_{X,Y} = H_Y + H_{X\mid Y}$ we have $H_{X\mid Y} = H_{X,Y} - H_Y$, so
     $$ I_{X;Y} = H_X - H_{X\mid Y} = H_X + H_Y - H_{X,Y} $$

** The KL-divergence is not symmetric
   * $\mathrm{KL}\bra{p\,\|\,q} = 0.5\log_2(0.5/0.9) + 0.5\log_2(0.5/0.1) \approx 0.737$ bits
   * $\mathrm{KL}\bra{q\,\|\,p} = 0.9\log_2(0.9/0.5) + 0.1\log_2(0.1/0.5) \approx 0.531$ bits

** KL-divergence between normals (harder)
   * With $p = \mathcal{N}(\mu_1,\sigma_1^2)$ and $q = \mathcal{N}(\mu_2,\sigma_2^2)$,
     $$ \log\!\bra{\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}} = \log\!\bra{\frac{\sigma_2}{\sigma_1}} - \frac{(x-\mu_1)^2}{2\,\sigma_1^2} + \frac{(x-\mu_2)^2}{2\,\sigma_2^2} $$
   * Take the expectation over $X\sim p$.  $\av{(X-\mu_1)^2} = \sigma_1^2$, and
     writing $X - \mu_2 = (X-\mu_1) + (\mu_1-\mu_2)$,
     $\av{(X-\mu_2)^2} = \sigma_1^2 + (\mu_1-\mu_2)^2$ because the cross
     term has zero mean
   * Hence
     $$ \mathrm{KL}\bra{p\,\|\,q} = \log\!\bra{\frac{\sigma_2}{\sigma_1}} - \frac{1}{2} + \frac{\sigma_1^2 + (\mu_1-\mu_2)^2}{2\,\sigma_2^2} $$
