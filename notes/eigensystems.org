* Main Points

** Eigen-Systems
   * We can understand ill-conditioning for linear regression by the
     eigen-decomposition of $\mat{M}=\mat{X}^\tr \mat{X}$
   * This should be revision
   * You know that an /eigenvector/, $\bm{v}$, satisfies $\mat{M}\,\bm{v}=\lambda\,\bm{v}$
   * For a symmetric matrix there are $n$ real orthogonal eigenvectors
   * You can prove they are orthogonal
   * *Orthogonal Matrices*
     * Putting the $n$ eigenvectors into a matrix $\mat{V}$ with
       columns $\bm{v}_i$ we obtain an orthogonal matrix
     * The defining property of an orthogonal matrix is
       $\mat{V}^\tr\mat{V} = \mat{V}\mat{V}^\tr=\mat{I}$
     * They correspond to rotations (with a possible reflection)
   * *Matrix Decomposition*
     * We can decompose a symmetric matrix, $\mat{M}$ as
       \begin{align*}
         \mat{M} = \mat{V}\,\mat{\Lambda}\,\mat{V}^\tr
       \end{align*}
     * Where $\mat{\Lambda}$ is a diagonal matrix of eigenvalues of $\mat{M}$ 
       (i.e. $\Lambda_{ii}=\lambda_i$)
     * $\mat{V}$ is the orthogonal matrix made up of the eigenvectors of $\mat{M}$
     * We can interpret the mapping of a symmetric matrix $\mat{M}$ as
       equivalent to
       1. a rotation defined by $\mat{V}^\tr$
       2. scaling of the $i^{th}$ component by $\lambda_i$ and
       3. a rotation backwards given by $\mat{V}$
     * Equivalently if we work in a coordinate system defined by the
       eigenvectors of $\mat{M}$ (this forms an orthonormal basis set)
       then we just rescale in the directions $\bm{v}_i$ by $\lambda_i$
       * A symmetric matrix just squashes or expands in different
         orthogonal directions (this is what the eigensystem captures)

   * *Inverse Matrices*
     * The inverse of a symmetric matrix $\mat{M}$ is given by
       \begin{align*}
         \mat{M}^{-1} = \mat{V}\,\mat{\Lambda}^{-1}\,\mat{V}^\tr
       \end{align*}
     * Where $\mat{\Lambda}^{-1}$ is a diagonal matrix with elements $\Lambda_{ii}^{-1}=1/\lambda_i$
     * This is only defined if all the eigenvalues of
       $\mat{M}$ are non-zero  ($\mat{M}$ is said to be full rank)
     * If $\lambda_i$ is very small then $1/\lambda_i$ is large and in
       taking the inverse $\mat{M}^{-1}\bm{x}$ any component of $\bm{x}$
       in the direction $\bm{v}_i$ will get magnified by $1/\lambda_i$
     * For linear regression we invert $\mat{M} = \mat{X}^\tr\mat{X}$
       * in directions where the training examples don't vary much the
	 associated eigenvalue will be small and the inverse inherently
	 unstable


* Exercises

1. Let $\mat{M}$ be a symmetric matrix with eigenvectors $\bm{v}_1$ and
   $\bm{v}_2$ and distinct eigenvalues $\lambda_1\neq\lambda_2$.  By
   computing $\bm{v}_2^\tr\mat{M}\,\bm{v}_1$ in two different ways, show
   that $\bm{v}_1$ and $\bm{v}_2$ are orthogonal
2. Show that the eigenvalues of $\mat{X}^\tr\mat{X}$ are never
   negative.  (Hint: consider $\len{\mat{X}\,\bm{v}}^2$)
3. Using $\mat{M} = \mat{V}\,\mat{\Lambda}\,\mat{V}^\tr$
   1. Show that $\mat{M}^{-1} = \mat{V}\,\mat{\Lambda}^{-1}\,\mat{V}^\tr$
      by multiplying the two together
   2. Show that $\mat{M}^k = \mat{V}\,\mat{\Lambda}^k\,\mat{V}^\tr$
4. Find the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of
   $\mat{M} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$ and check
   that $\mat{M} = \mat{V}\,\mat{\Lambda}\,\mat{V}^\tr$

* Experiments

** Eigensystems
   * In either Matlab/Octave or python generate random matrices and check
     the matrix identities

#+BEGIN_SRC matlab
X = randn(5,4)  % generate a mock design matrix with 5 inputs of length 4
M = X'*X        % compute a symmetric matrix
[V,L] = eig(M)  % compute eigenvectors and eigenvalues
V*L*V'          % should be identical to M
V*V'            % should be the identity matrix (up to rounding precision)
V'*V            % should be the identity matrix (up to rounding precision)
x = randn(4,1)  % generate a random column matrix of length 4
y = randn(4,1)  % generate another random column matrix of length 4
xp = V*x        % apply V to x
yp = V*y        % apply V to y
norm(x)         % compute Euclidean norm of x
norm(xp)        % should be the same as the Euclidean norm of x
x'*y            % compute inner product of x and y
xp'*yp          % inner product of xp and yp (should be the same as above)

Z = rand(4,5)   % a design matrix with more unknowns than examples
W = Z'*Z        % a covariance-like matrix (but without subtracting the mean)
eig(W)          % compute eigenvalues (one should be 0 up to machine precision)

* Answers

1. On one hand $\bm{v}_2^\tr(\mat{M}\,\bm{v}_1) = \lambda_1\,\bm{v}_2^\tr\bm{v}_1$.
   On the other, as $\mat{M}^\tr=\mat{M}$,
   $\bm{v}_2^\tr\mat{M}\,\bm{v}_1 = (\mat{M}\,\bm{v}_2)^\tr\bm{v}_1
   = \lambda_2\,\bm{v}_2^\tr\bm{v}_1$.  Subtracting,
   $(\lambda_1-\lambda_2)\,\bm{v}_2^\tr\bm{v}_1 = 0$ and since
   $\lambda_1\neq\lambda_2$ we must have $\bm{v}_2^\tr\bm{v}_1=0$
2. If $\mat{X}^\tr\mat{X}\,\bm{v} = \lambda\,\bm{v}$ then
   $\lambda\,\len{\bm{v}}^2 = \bm{v}^\tr\mat{X}^\tr\mat{X}\,\bm{v}
   = \len{\mat{X}\,\bm{v}}^2 \geq 0$, so $\lambda\geq0$.  It is zero
   exactly when $\mat{X}\,\bm{v}=\bm{0}$: a direction the data say
   nothing about
3. Matrix powers
   1. $\mat{V}\,\mat{\Lambda}\,\mat{V}^\tr\,\mat{V}\,\mat{\Lambda}^{-1}\mat{V}^\tr
      = \mat{V}\,\mat{\Lambda}\,\mat{\Lambda}^{-1}\mat{V}^\tr
      = \mat{V}\,\mat{V}^\tr = \mat{I}$, using $\mat{V}^\tr\mat{V}=\mat{I}$
      twice
   2. In $\mat{M}^k = \mat{V}\,\mat{\Lambda}\,\mat{V}^\tr\,\mat{V}\,\mat{\Lambda}\,\mat{V}^\tr
      \cdots\mat{V}\,\mat{\Lambda}\,\mat{V}^\tr$ every inner
      $\mat{V}^\tr\mat{V}$ is $\mat{I}$, leaving
      $\mat{V}\,\mat{\Lambda}^k\,\mat{V}^\tr$
4. $\det(\mat{M}-\lambda\mat{I}) = (2-\lambda)^2-1 = 0$ gives
   $\lambda=3$ with $\bm{v}=(1,1)^\tr/\sqrt{2}$ and $\lambda=1$ with
   $\bm{v}=(1,-1)^\tr/\sqrt{2}$.  Then
   \begin{align*}
     \frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}
     \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}
     \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}
     = \frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix} 4 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{pmatrix} = \mat{M}
   \end{align*}
