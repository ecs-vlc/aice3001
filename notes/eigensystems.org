* Main Points

** Eigen-Systems
   * We can understand ill-conditioning for linear regression by the
     eigen-decomposition of $\mat{M}=\mat{X}^\tr \mat{X}$
   * This should be revision
   * You know that an /eigenvector/, $\bm{v}$, satisfies $\mat{M}\,\bm{v}=\lambda\,\bm{v}$
   * For a symmetric matrix there are $n$ real orthogonal eigenvectors
   * You can prove they are orthogonal
   * *Orthogonal Matrices*
     * Putting the $n$ eigenvectors into a matrix $\mat{V}$ with
       columns $\bm{v}_i$ we obtain an orthogonal matrix
     * The defining property of an orthogonal matrix is
       $\mat{V}^\tr\mat{V} = \mat{V}\mat{V}^\tr=\mat{I}$
     * They correspond to rotations (with a possible reflection): they
       preserve lengths and angles, since
       $(\mat{V}\bm{x})^\tr(\mat{V}\bm{y}) = \bm{x}^\tr\mat{V}^\tr\mat{V}\,\bm{y}
       = \bm{x}^\tr\bm{y}$
     * $\mat{V}^\tr\mat{V}=\mat{I}$ follows directly from the eigenvectors
       being orthonormal.  $\mat{V}\mat{V}^\tr=\mat{I}$ then follows
       because $\mat{V}$ is invertible: if $\mat{V}\bm{x}=\bm{0}$ then
       $\bm{x}=\mat{V}^\tr\mat{V}\bm{x}=\bm{0}$
   * *Matrix Decomposition*
     * We can decompose a symmetric matrix, $\mat{M}$ as
       \begin{align*}
         \mat{M} = \mat{V}\,\mat{\Lambda}\,\mat{V}^\tr
       \end{align*}
     * Where $\mat{\Lambda}$ is a diagonal matrix of eigenvalues of $\mat{M}$ 
       (i.e. $\Lambda_{ii}=\lambda_i$)
     * $\mat{V}$ is the orthogonal matrix made up of the eigenvectors of $\mat{M}$
     * We can interpret the mapping of a symmetric matrix $\mat{M}$ as
       equivalent to
       1. a rotation defined by $\mat{V}^\tr$
       2. scaling of the $i^{th}$ component by $\lambda_i$ and
       3. a rotation backwards given by $\mat{V}$
     * Equivalently if we work in a coordinate system defined by the
       eigenvectors of $\mat{M}$ (this forms an orthonormal basis set)
       then we just rescale in the directions $\bm{v}_i$ by $\lambda_i$
       * A symmetric matrix just squashes or expands in different
         orthogonal directions (this is what the eigensystem captures)

   * *Inverse Matrices*
     * The inverse of a symmetric matrix $\mat{M}$ is given by
       \begin{align*}
         \mat{M}^{-1} = \mat{V}\,\mat{\Lambda}^{-1}\,\mat{V}^\tr
       \end{align*}
     * Where $\mat{\Lambda}^{-1}$ is a diagonal matrix with elements $\Lambda_{ii}^{-1}=1/\lambda_i$
     * This is only defined if all the eigenvalues of
       $\mat{M}$ are non-zero  ($\mat{M}$ is said to be full rank)
     * If $\lambda_i$ is very small then $1/\lambda_i$ is large and in
       taking the inverse $\mat{M}^{-1}\bm{x}$ any component of $\bm{x}$
       in the direction $\bm{v}_i$ will get magnified by $1/\lambda_i$
     * For linear regression we invert $\mat{M} = \mat{X}^\tr\mat{X}$
       * in directions where the training examples don't vary much the
	 associated eigenvalue will be small and the inverse inherently
	 unstable

** Condition Number
   * Suppose we solve $\mat{M}\,\bm{x} = \bm{b}$ for a symmetric,
     invertible $\mat{M}$, but $\bm{b}$ carries an error $\delta\bm{b}$.
     The solution then carries an error
     $\delta\bm{x} = \mat{M}^{-1}\delta\bm{b}$
   * In the eigenvector basis $\mat{M}^{-1}$ just multiplies the
     component along $\bm{v}_i$ by $1/\lambda_i$, so
     \begin{align*}
       \len{\delta\bm{x}} \leq \frac{\len{\delta\bm{b}}}{|\lambda_{\min}|}
       \qquad\text{and}\qquad
       \len{\bm{b}} = \len{\mat{M}\,\bm{x}} \leq |\lambda_{\max}|\,\len{\bm{x}}
     \end{align*}
     Rearranging the second as
     $1/\len{\bm{x}} \leq |\lambda_{\max}|/\len{\bm{b}}$ and
     multiplying it by the first gives
     \begin{align*}
       \frac{\len{\delta\bm{x}}}{\len{\bm{x}}} \leq
       \kappa\, \frac{\len{\delta\bm{b}}}{\len{\bm{b}}},
       \qquad
       \kappa = \frac{|\lambda_{\max}|}{|\lambda_{\min}|}
     \end{align*}
   * $\kappa$ is the /condition number/.  A relative error in the data
     can be amplified by up to a factor $\kappa$ in the solution
   * For a symmetric matrix $|\lambda_{\max}|$ is the spectral norm
     $\len{\mat{M}}_2$ from the vector spaces lecture, and
     $|\lambda_{\min}|^{-1} = \len{\mat{M}^{-1}}_2$, so
     $\kappa = \len{\mat{M}}_2\,\len{\mat{M}^{-1}}_2$
   * Example: in the mappings lecture
     $\mat{X} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1.001 \end{pmatrix}$ has
     eigenvalues $\approx 2.0005$ and $0.0005$, so $\kappa\approx 4000$.
     Changing $y_2$ by $0.001$ is a relative change of $3.5\times10^{-4}$
     in $\bm{y}$, but changed $\bm{w}$ from $(1,1)$ to $(0,2)$, a
     relative change of $1$: an amplification of about $2800$, within
     the bound

** Rank and Null Space
   * Writing out $\mat{M} = \mat{V}\,\mat{\Lambda}\,\mat{V}^\tr$ column by
     column gives the useful form
     \begin{align*}
       \mat{M} = \sum_{i=1}^n \lambda_i\, \bm{v}_i\,\bm{v}_i^\tr
     \end{align*}
     a sum of $n$ rank-one pieces, one per eigenvector
   * The /rank/ of a symmetric matrix is its number of non-zero
     eigenvalues.  (For a general square matrix the rank is the number
     of linearly independent columns; the two agree for symmetric
     matrices but not in general)
   * The eigenvectors with zero eigenvalue span the /null space/: any
     $\bm{x} = \sum_k a_k\,\bm{v}_k$ built from them has
     $\mat{M}\,\bm{x} = \bm{0}$
   * A matrix is /rank deficient/ if it has a zero eigenvalue.
     Equivalently its columns are linearly dependent, or
     $\det(\mat{M}) = \prod_i\lambda_i = 0$.  It then has no inverse:
     if $\bm{x}$ solves $\mat{M}\,\bm{x}=\bm{b}$ then so does $\bm{x}$
     plus anything in the null space

** Pseudo-Inverse of a Symmetric Matrix
   * If $\mat{M}$ has $k$ non-zero eigenvalues $\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_k$
     we define
     \begin{align*}
       \mat{M}^+ = \mat{V}\,\mat{\Lambda}^+\mat{V}^\tr
       = \sum_{i=1}^k \frac{1}{\lambda_i}\, \bm{v}_i\,\bm{v}_i^\tr,
       \qquad
       \mat{\Lambda}^+ = \mathrm{diag}\Big(\tfrac{1}{\lambda_1},\ldots,
       \tfrac{1}{\lambda_k}, 0,\ldots,0\Big)
     \end{align*}
     i.e. invert the non-zero eigenvalues and leave the zero ones at zero
   * If $\mat{M}\,\bm{x}=\bm{b}$ has solutions, $\bm{x} = \mat{M}^+\bm{b}$
     is the one with no component in the null space.  Since every other
     solution adds a null-space vector, which is orthogonal to it, this
     is the solution with the smallest $\len{\bm{x}}$
   * If $\bm{b}$ has a component in the null space there is no exact
     solution, and $\mat{M}^+\bm{b}$ minimises $\len{\mat{M}\,\bm{x}-\bm{b}}$
     instead
   * This is the symmetric case of the Moore--Penrose pseudo-inverse
     described in the SVD notes

** Low Rank Approximation
   * Keeping only the $k$ largest (in magnitude) eigenvalues gives the
     rank-$k$ approximation
     $\mat{M}_k = \sum_{i=1}^k \lambda_i\,\bm{v}_i\,\bm{v}_i^\tr$
     * It needs only $k$ eigenvalues and $k$ eigenvectors, $k(n+1)$
       numbers rather than $n^2$
     * The error, $\mat{M}-\mat{M}_k$, is made of the discarded terms,
       so it is small when the discarded eigenvalues are small
   * For an ill-conditioned matrix we can set the tiny eigenvalues to
     zero and use the pseudo-inverse.  The directions that would have
     amplified noise by $1/\lambda_i$ are simply ignored
     * This trades a little bias (we lose whatever true signal lay in
       those directions) for a large reduction in variance
     * The experiment below shows it in action.  Ridge regression, in
       the SVD lecture, is a smoother version of the same idea: it damps
       small eigenvalues rather than discarding them

** Non-Normal Matrices
   * Symmetric matrices belong to a larger class, the /normal/
     matrices, which satisfy
     $\mat{M}\,\mat{M}^\dagger = \mat{M}^\dagger\mat{M}$, where
     $\mat{M}^\dagger = (\mat{M}^\tr)^*$ is the conjugate transpose (for
     real matrices just $\mat{M}^\tr$)
     * Normal matrices are exactly the matrices with a full set of
       orthonormal (possibly complex) eigenvectors
     * Hermitian matrices ($\mat{M}=\mat{M}^\dagger$) are normal; they
       matter in quantum theory, much less in machine learning
     * A real matrix can be normal without being symmetric: the
       rotation $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ is
       normal, with eigenvalues $\pm\mathrm{i}$
     * Most non-symmetric real matrices are not normal, and their
       eigenvectors behave less nicely
   * *Complex eigenvalues.*  The eigenvalues are the roots of the
     /characteristic equation/ $\det(\mat{M}-\lambda\,\mat{I})=0$.
     $(\mat{M}-\lambda\,\mat{I})\,\bm{v}=\bm{0}$ can only have a non-zero
     solution if $\mat{M}-\lambda\,\mat{I}$ is singular.  This is a
     polynomial of order $n$ in $\lambda$, so there are $n$ roots
     (counting repeats), and in general they are complex
     * If $\mat{M}$ is real and $\lambda$ is complex then $\bm{v}$ is
       complex too.  Taking the complex conjugate of
       $\mat{M}\,\bm{v}=\lambda\,\bm{v}$ gives
       $\mat{M}\,\bm{v}^*=\lambda^*\,\bm{v}^*$, so complex eigenvalues and
       eigenvectors come in conjugate pairs
   * *Defective matrices.*  A non-symmetric matrix need not have $n$
     linearly independent eigenvectors; if it does not it is called
     /defective/
     * For example $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ has the
       repeated eigenvalue $1$ but only one eigenvector, $(1,0)^\tr$
     * A randomly generated matrix is almost never defective, but
       defective matrices do turn up in structured problems
   * *Left and right eigenvectors.*  As well as the usual (right)
     eigenvectors $\mat{M}\,\bm{v}_i=\lambda_i\,\bm{v}_i$ there are left
     eigenvectors $\bm{u}_i^\tr\mat{M} = \lambda_i\,\bm{u}_i^\tr$, i.e.
     eigenvectors of $\mat{M}^\tr$.  As
     $\det(\mat{M}^\tr-\lambda\,\mat{I}) = \det(\mat{M}-\lambda\,\mat{I})$
     they have the same eigenvalues
     * For a symmetric matrix the left eigenvectors are just the
       transposes of the right ones
     * In general they differ, and the right eigenvectors are not
       orthogonal to one another.  Instead the two sets are
       /biorthogonal/: computing $\bm{u}_i^\tr\mat{M}\,\bm{v}_j$ in two
       ways (exactly as in the proof for symmetric matrices) gives
       $(\lambda_i-\lambda_j)\,\bm{u}_i^\tr\bm{v}_j = 0$, so
       $\bm{u}_i^\tr\bm{v}_j=0$ whenever $\lambda_i\neq\lambda_j$
   * *Eigen-decomposition.*  If $\mat{M}$ is not defective, put the
     right eigenvectors in the columns of $\mat{V}$.  Then
     $\mat{M}\,\mat{V} = \mat{V}\,\mat{\Lambda}$ and, as the columns are
     independent, $\mat{V}$ is invertible, so
     \begin{align*}
       \mat{M} = \mat{V}\,\mat{\Lambda}\,\mat{V}^{-1}
     \end{align*}
     * $\mat{V}$ is no longer orthogonal, so $\mat{V}^{-1}\neq\mat{V}^\tr$
     * Multiplying $\mat{M}=\mat{V}\mat{\Lambda}\mat{V}^{-1}$ on the left
       by $\mat{V}^{-1}$ gives $\mat{V}^{-1}\mat{M} =
       \mat{\Lambda}\,\mat{V}^{-1}$: the rows of $\mat{V}^{-1}$ are the
       left eigenvectors, scaled so that $\bm{u}_i^\tr\bm{v}_i=1$
     * We still get $\mat{M}^k = \mat{V}\,\mat{\Lambda}^k\,\mat{V}^{-1}$,
       but the geometric picture (rotate, stretch along orthogonal axes,
       rotate back) is lost.  This is one reason we lean on the singular
       value decomposition, which works for every matrix

** Non-Normal Matrices
   * In python, look at the eigensystem of a random non-symmetric matrix

#+BEGIN_SRC python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(2)
M = rng.normal(size=(4, 4))       # not symmetric
lam, V = np.linalg.eig(M)         # columns of V are right eigenvectors
print(lam)                        # complex eigenvalues come in conjugate pairs
print(np.allclose(M, V @ np.diag(lam) @ np.linalg.inv(V)))   # M = V L V^-1
U = np.linalg.inv(V)              # rows should be left eigenvectors
print(np.round(U @ V, 10))        # u_i^T v_j: the identity (biorthogonal)
print(np.round(V.conj().T @ V, 3))  # but the v_i are not orthogonal
#+END_SRC

** Low Rank Pseudo-Inverse
   * Regression with two almost identical features: compare inverting
     every eigenvalue of $\mat{X}^\tr\mat{X}$ with dropping the tiny one

#+BEGIN_SRC python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
m = 50
X = rng.normal(size=(m, 3))
X[:, 2] = X[:, 1] + 1e-3 * rng.normal(size=m)   # feature 3 is almost feature 2
w_true = np.array([1.0, 2.0, 0.0])
C = X.T @ X                                      # symmetric 3x3
lam, V = np.linalg.eigh(C)                       # eigenvalues in ascending order
print(lam, lam[-1] / lam[0])                     # tiny eigenvalue; condition no.
for trial in range(3):                           # same X, fresh noise each time
    y = X @ w_true + 0.1 * rng.normal(size=m)
    w_full = V @ np.diag(1 / lam) @ V.T @ X.T @ y  # invert all eigenvalues
    lam_plus = np.where(lam > 1e-2 * lam[-1], 1 / lam, 0)  # drop the tiny one
    w_trunc = V @ np.diag(lam_plus) @ V.T @ X.T @ y
    print(np.round(w_full, 2), np.round(w_trunc, 2))
#+END_SRC

   * The condition number is about $3\times10^6$.  With every eigenvalue
     inverted the last two weights jump around from one noise sample to
     the next (e.g. $(6.27,-4.26)$ then $(-1.38, 3.36)$), though their sum
     stays near 2.  Dropping the tiny eigenvalue gives about $(1,1)$
     every time
   * Neither recovers the true $(2,0)$: the data cannot tell the two
     features apart.  The truncated answer splits the weight equally,
     the minimum-norm choice, and predicts just as well

* Answers

1. On one hand $\bm{v}_2^\tr(\mat{M}\,\bm{v}_1) = \lambda_1\,\bm{v}_2^\tr\bm{v}_1$.
   On the other, as $\mat{M}^\tr=\mat{M}$,
   $\bm{v}_2^\tr\mat{M}\,\bm{v}_1 = (\mat{M}\,\bm{v}_2)^\tr\bm{v}_1
   = \lambda_2\,\bm{v}_2^\tr\bm{v}_1$.  Subtracting,
   $(\lambda_1-\lambda_2)\,\bm{v}_2^\tr\bm{v}_1 = 0$ and since
   $\lambda_1\neq\lambda_2$ we must have $\bm{v}_2^\tr\bm{v}_1=0$
2. If $\mat{X}^\tr\mat{X}\,\bm{v} = \lambda\,\bm{v}$ then
   $\lambda\,\len{\bm{v}}^2 = \bm{v}^\tr\mat{X}^\tr\mat{X}\,\bm{v}
   = \len{\mat{X}\,\bm{v}}^2 \geq 0$, so $\lambda\geq0$.  It is zero
   exactly when $\mat{X}\,\bm{v}=\bm{0}$: a direction the data say
   nothing about
3. Matrix powers
   1. $\mat{V}\,\mat{\Lambda}\,\mat{V}^\tr\,\mat{V}\,\mat{\Lambda}^{-1}\mat{V}^\tr
      = \mat{V}\,\mat{\Lambda}\,\mat{\Lambda}^{-1}\mat{V}^\tr
      = \mat{V}\,\mat{V}^\tr = \mat{I}$, using $\mat{V}^\tr\mat{V}=\mat{I}$
      twice
   2. In $\mat{M}^k = \mat{V}\,\mat{\Lambda}\,\mat{V}^\tr\,\mat{V}\,\mat{\Lambda}\,\mat{V}^\tr
      \cdots\mat{V}\,\mat{\Lambda}\,\mat{V}^\tr$ every inner
      $\mat{V}^\tr\mat{V}$ is $\mat{I}$, leaving
      $\mat{V}\,\mat{\Lambda}^k\,\mat{V}^\tr$
4. $\det(\mat{M}-\lambda\mat{I}) = (2-\lambda)^2-1 = 0$ gives
   $\lambda=3$ with $\bm{v}=(1,1)^\tr/\sqrt{2}$ and $\lambda=1$ with
   $\bm{v}=(1,-1)^\tr/\sqrt{2}$.  Then
   \begin{align*}
     \frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}
     \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}
     \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}
     = \frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix} 4 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{pmatrix} = \mat{M}
   \end{align*}
5. Non-normal matrices
   1. $\mat{M}$ is upper triangular, so $\det(\mat{M}-\lambda\mat{I}) =
      (1-\lambda)(3-\lambda)$ and the eigenvalues are $1$ and $3$.  Right
      eigenvectors: $\bm{v}_1 = (1,0)^\tr$ and $\bm{v}_2 = (1,1)^\tr$.
      Left eigenvectors (eigenvectors of $\mat{M}^\tr$):
      $\bm{u}_1 = (1,-1)^\tr$ and $\bm{u}_2 = (0,1)^\tr$.  Then
      $\bm{v}_1^\tr\bm{v}_2 = 1 \neq 0$, while $\bm{u}_1^\tr\bm{v}_2 =
      1-1 = 0$ and $\bm{u}_2^\tr\bm{v}_1 = 0$.  (With these choices
      $\bm{u}_1^\tr\bm{v}_1 = \bm{u}_2^\tr\bm{v}_2 = 1$ already.)
   2. $\mat{V} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$,
      $\mat{V}^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$,
      whose rows are $\bm{u}_1^\tr$ and $\bm{u}_2^\tr$, and
      \begin{align*}
        \mat{V}\,\mat{\Lambda}\,\mat{V}^{-1} =
        \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}
        \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}
        \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}
        = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}
        \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}
        = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix} = \mat{M}
      \end{align*}
   3. The only eigenvalue is $1$ (twice).  An eigenvector must satisfy
      $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\bm{v} = \bm{0}$, i.e.
      $v_2 = 0$, so every eigenvector is a multiple of $(1,0)^\tr$
   4. $\mat{R}\,\mat{R}^\tr = \mat{R}^\tr\mat{R} = \mat{I}$ (it is a
      rotation by $90^\circ$), but $\mat{R}^\tr \neq \mat{R}$.
      $\mat{R}\,(1,-\mathrm{i})^\tr = (\mathrm{i}, 1)^\tr =
      \mathrm{i}\,(1,-\mathrm{i})^\tr$.  The other eigenvector is the
      complex conjugate, $(1,\mathrm{i})^\tr$, with eigenvalue
      $-\mathrm{i}$.  As $\mat{R}$ is normal these two are orthogonal,
      using the complex inner product (conjugate the first vector):
      $(1,\mathrm{i})^\dagger (1,-\mathrm{i})^\tr = 1\cdot1 +
      (-\mathrm{i})(-\mathrm{i}) = 1 - 1 = 0$
6. $\mat{V}\,\mat{\Lambda}$ has columns $\lambda_i\bm{v}_i$, and a matrix
   with columns $\bm{a}_i$ times one with rows $\bm{v}_i^\tr$ is
   $\sum_i \bm{a}_i\bm{v}_i^\tr$.  So $\mat{V}\,\mat{\Lambda}\,\mat{V}^\tr
   = \sum_i\lambda_i\,\bm{v}_i\bm{v}_i^\tr$.  Then
   $\mat{M}\,\bm{v}_j = \sum_i \lambda_i\,\bm{v}_i\,(\bm{v}_i^\tr\bm{v}_j)
   = \lambda_j\,\bm{v}_j$, since $\bm{v}_i^\tr\bm{v}_j = 0$ unless $i=j$
7. Rank-deficient example
   1. $\lambda=2$ with $\bm{v}_1 = (1,1)^\tr/\sqrt{2}$, and $\lambda=0$
      with $\bm{v}_2 = (1,-1)^\tr/\sqrt{2}$.  The rank is 1 and the null
      space is all multiples of $(1,-1)^\tr$
   2. $\mat{M}^+ = \tfrac{1}{2}\bm{v}_1\bm{v}_1^\tr =
      \tfrac{1}{4}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$
   3. The solutions are $x_1+x_2 = 2$, i.e. $(1,1)^\tr + t\,(1,-1)^\tr$
      for any $t$, with $\len{\bm{x}}^2 = 2 + 2t^2$.  This is smallest at
      $t=0$, and $\mat{M}^+(2,2)^\tr = \tfrac14(4,4)^\tr = (1,1)^\tr$
   4. $\mat{M}^+(1,3)^\tr = \tfrac14(4,4)^\tr = (1,1)^\tr$, giving
      $\mat{M}\,\bm{x} = (2,2)^\tr$.  That is the closest point to
      $(1,3)^\tr$ that $\mat{M}$ can reach: the projection of $(1,3)^\tr$
      onto $(1,1)^\tr$
8. Equality needs both inequalities to be tight: $\bm{x}$ along the
   eigenvector of $\lambda_{\max}$, so that
   $\len{\mat{M}\bm{x}} = |\lambda_{\max}|\len{\bm{x}}$, and $\delta\bm{b}$ along the eigenvector
   of $\lambda_{\min}$.  So take $\bm{b} = \bm{v}_{\max}$ and
   $\delta\bm{b} = \epsilon\,\bm{v}_{\min}$: the signal lies in the
