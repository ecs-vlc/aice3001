* Main Points

** Inverse Problems
   * Much of machine learning can be viewed as solving an inverse problem
   * We collect data about the world by performing a series of measurements
   * Our task is to infer properties of the world from the data
   * In linear regression we have $m$ observations $(\bm{x}_k, y_k)$
     and want $p$ unknown weights $\bm{w}$ such that
     $y_k = \bm{x}_k^\tr\,\bm{w}$
   * Stacking the feature vectors $\bm{x}_k^\tr$ as the rows of the
     /design matrix/ $\mat{X}$ (an $m\times p$ matrix) we can write this as
     $\bm{y} = \mat{X}\,\bm{w}$
   * If $m=p$ and $\mat{X}$ is invertible then $\bm{w} =
     \mat{X}^{-1}\bm{y}$, but this is rarely the situation we are in

** Least Squares (Over-Constrained Problems)
   * We can have contradictory data that our model cannot explain
   * This may arise because
     * We have errors in the data
     * Our data contains insufficient information
     * Our model is too simple
   * If we have more training data than free parameters ($m>p$) this
     is likely to occur
   * We typically solve this by introducing a loss function we
     minimise.  The classic example is the squared loss
     \begin{align*}
       L(\bm{w}) = \sum_{k=1}^m \left(\bm{x}_k^\tr\bm{w} - y_k\right)^2
       = \len{\mat{X}\,\bm{w} - \bm{y}}^2
     \end{align*}
     This was introduced by Gauss to predict the orbit of the asteroid Ceres
   * Setting the gradient to zero (see Exercise 1) gives
     \begin{align*}
       \bm{w} = \left(\mat{X}^\tr\mat{X}\right)^{-1}\mat{X}^\tr\bm{y}
       = \mat{X}^+\bm{y}
     \end{align*}
     where $\mat{X}^+ = (\mat{X}^\tr\mat{X})^{-1}\mat{X}^\tr$ is the pseudo-inverse
   * This requires $\mat{X}^\tr\mat{X}$ to be invertible, which
     is the case when the columns of $\mat{X}$ are linearly independent

** Under-Constrained Problems
   * We can also be in a situation when many models (learning
     machines) explain the data
   * This will typically happen when we have more free parameters than
     data ($m<p$), or when we are unlucky and the training examples
     don't span the full space
   * In linear regression $\mat{X}^\tr\mat{X}$ is then singular (see
     Exercise 3), so the pseudo-inverse formula fails.  The problem is
     said to be /ill-posed/
   * As an example, consider fitting a polynomial of degree 5,
     $y = w_0 + w_1\,x + \cdots + w_5\,x^5$ (6 parameters), to 4
     data points $(x_i,y_i)$.  If $p_0(x)$ is the cubic through the
     four points then
     \begin{align*}
       p(x) = p_0(x) + (a + b\,x)\,(x-x_1)(x-x_2)(x-x_3)(x-x_4)
     \end{align*}
     passes through all four points for /every/ choice of $a$ and $b$
     (Exercise 4).  We have 6 unknowns and 4 equations, leaving 2 free
     directions.  The figure shows five members of this family.  They
     agree near the data but make wildly different predictions away
     from it
     #+ATTR_LATEX: :width 0.6\textwidth
     [[file:figures/underConstrainedCurves-5.pdf]]
   * Here we have to choose a particular model.  To do this requires
     (implicitly or explicitly) making additional assumptions
   * Ockham's razor suggests choosing the "simplest" solution, although
     what counts as simplest is a choice we make
   * For linear regression, adding an $L_2$ regulariser
     $\eta\,\len{\bm{w}}^2$ to the loss makes the solution unique.  We
     will see exactly how this works when we meet the singular value
     decomposition
   * For high-dimensional inputs we can be over-constrained
     in some directions and under-constrained in others

** Ill-Conditioning
   * Even when we are not under-constrained our inverse can be very
     sensitive to the data
   * That is, small errors can be strongly magnified
   * A matrix that is close to singular is ill-conditioned: some of its
     eigenvalues are tiny compared with the largest, so it squashes
     space towards a lower-dimensional subspace.  Inverting it
     stretches those directions back out, magnifying any noise in them
   * A worked example: take two data points with two nearly identical features
     \begin{align*}
       \mat{X} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1.001 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad
       \bm{y} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 2.001 + \delta \end{pmatrix}
     \end{align*}
     Solving $\mat{X}\,\bm{w}=\bm{y}$, subtracting the first equation
     from the second gives $0.001\,w_2 = 0.001 + \delta$, so
     \begin{align*}
       \bm{w} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 - 1000\,\delta \\ 1 + 1000\,\delta \end{pmatrix}
     \end{align*}
     * A change $\delta$ in the data produces a change $1000\,\delta$ in
       the weights.  With $\delta = 0.001$ (a 0.05% change in $y_2$) the
       weights jump from $(1,1)$ to $(0,2)$ and the prediction for a
       new input $\bm{x}=(1,0)^\tr$ goes from 1 to 0
     * The weights always move along $(-1,1)^\tr$.  This is (very
       nearly) the eigenvector of $\mat{X}$ with the small eigenvalue
       ($\approx 0.0005$, against $\approx 2$ for the other one)
     * The data hardly constrain the weights in this direction: the two
       features are so alike that the data cannot tell us how to share
       the weight between them
   * Common causes of ill-conditioning in machine learning
     * Roughly as many data points as parameters
     * Nearly collinear features (e.g. floor area and number of rooms)
     * Polynomial features $x, x^2, \ldots, x^9$ for $x\in[0,1]$, which
       all look alike (see the experiments)
     * Features on very different scales (easily fixed by standardising
       each feature to zero mean and unit variance)
   * Ill-conditioning leads to high variance in the bias-variance sense
     and hence poor generalisation
   * Regularisers are one way of improving the conditioning

** Least Squares in Practice
   * The formula $\bm{w} = (\mat{X}^\tr\mat{X})^{-1}\mat{X}^\tr\bm{y}$
     is for understanding, not for computing
   * Use a least squares solver such as =np.linalg.lstsq(X, y)=, which
     works with $\mat{X}$ directly
   * Forming $\mat{X}^\tr\mat{X}$ squares the ratio of the largest to
     smallest eigenvalues (the /condition number/, defined properly in
     the next lecture).  If that ratio is $10^8$ for $\mat{X}$ it becomes
     $10^{16}$ for $\mat{X}^\tr\mat{X}$, beyond the roughly 16
     significant figures of a double-precision number
   * When $m<p$, =lstsq= returns the solution with the smallest
     $\len{\bm{w}}$, which is one version of the "simplest" solution.
     We will see why when we meet the singular value decomposition

** Three Regimes
   | Regime                      | Exact fit?  | Unique?       | Method                               |
   |-----------------------------+-------------+---------------+--------------------------------------|
   | $m>p$ (over-constrained)    | usually not | yes (usually) | minimise $\len{\mat{X}\bm{w}-\bm{y}}^2$ |
   | $m=p$                       | yes         | yes (usually) | $\bm{w}=\mat{X}^{-1}\bm{y}$          |
   | $m<p$ (under-constrained)   | yes, many   | no            | choose the "simplest"                |
   * "Usually" means provided the columns of $\mat{X}$ are linearly
     independent
   * Real problems mix these: over-constrained in some directions and
     (nearly) under-constrained in others.  The nearly under-constrained
     directions are what make a problem ill-conditioned

* Experiments

** Ill-Conditioning and Under-Constrained Systems
   * Using python (with numpy), try the following and check you
     understand the output

#+BEGIN_SRC python
import numpy as np

# 1. The worked example: a tiny change in y, a big change in w
X = np.array([[1, 1], [1, 1.001]])
for y2 in [2.001, 2.002, 2.0005]:
    print(y2, np.linalg.solve(X, [2, y2]))
print(np.linalg.eigh(X))   # eigenvalues and eigenvectors (X is symmetric)

# 2. Polynomial features are ill-conditioned
x = np.linspace(0, 1, 50)
for degree in [1, 3, 5, 9]:
    Xp = np.vander(x, degree + 1, increasing=True)  # columns 1, x, x^2, ...
    print(degree, np.linalg.cond(Xp), np.linalg.cond(Xp.T @ Xp))

# 3. An under-constrained system: 3 equations, 6 unknowns
rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
X = rng.normal(size=(3, 6))
y = rng.normal(size=3)
w, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(X, y)
print(np.allclose(X @ w, y))                   # fits the data exactly
print(np.linalg.eigvalsh(X.T @ X))             # three (numerically) zero eigenvalues
v = np.linalg.svd(X)[2][-1]                    # a direction with X v = 0
print(np.allclose(X @ (w + 5 * v), y))         # ...so this also fits exactly
print(np.linalg.norm(w), np.linalg.norm(w + 5 * v))  # but a larger norm
#+END_SRC

   * In part 2, =np.linalg.cond= computes the condition number
     (covered next lecture).  Notice that the condition number of
     $\mat{X}^\tr\mat{X}$ is the square of that of $\mat{X}$, reaching
     about $10^{13}$ for degree 9
   * In part 3, try adding other multiples of =v=.  Why can the norm
     never be smaller than that of =w=?

* Answers

1. Using $\len{\bm{a}}^2 = \bm{a}^\tr\bm{a}$ and
   $\bm{w}^\tr\mat{X}^\tr\bm{y} = \bm{y}^\tr\mat{X}\,\bm{w}$ (a number
   equals its own transpose)
   \begin{align*}
     \len{\mat{X}\,\bm{w} - \bm{y}}^2
     = \bm{w}^\tr\mat{X}^\tr\mat{X}\,\bm{w} - 2\,\bm{w}^\tr\mat{X}^\tr\bm{y}
       + \bm{y}^\tr\bm{y}
   \end{align*}
   As $\mat{X}^\tr\mat{X}$ is symmetric, Exercise 2 gives the gradient
   $2\,(\mat{X}^\tr\mat{X}\,\bm{w} - \mat{X}^\tr\bm{y})$.  Setting this
   to zero gives $\mat{X}^\tr\mat{X}\,\bm{w} = \mat{X}^\tr\bm{y}$ and, if
   $\mat{X}^\tr\mat{X}$ is invertible, the result
2. $\displaystyle \pd{}{w_k}\sum_{i,j} w_i\,M_{ij}\,w_j
   = \sum_j M_{kj}\,w_j + \sum_i w_i\,M_{ik}
   = (\mat{M}\,\bm{w})_k + (\mat{M}^\tr\bm{w})_k$
3. The $p$ columns of $\mat{X}$ are vectors in $\mathbb{R}^m$ with
   $m<p$, so they are linearly dependent: some non-zero $\bm{v}$ has
   $\mat{X}\,\bm{v}=\bm{0}$.  Then $\mat{X}^\tr\mat{X}\,\bm{v} = \bm{0}$,
   so $\bm{v}$ is an eigenvector of $\mat{X}^\tr\mat{X}$ with eigenvalue 0
   and $\mat{X}^\tr\mat{X}$ has no inverse.  Any multiple of $\bm{v}$
   can be added to a solution without changing the fit
4. Polynomial example
   1. At each data point one factor $(x-x_i)$ vanishes, so
      $p(x_i) = p_0(x_i) = y_i$.  The degree is at most
      $1 + 4 = 5$
   2. If $r(x)$ is another such polynomial then $r(x)-p_0(x)$ has
      degree at most 5 and is zero at $x_1,\ldots,x_4$, so it is
      divisible by $(x-x_1)\cdots(x-x_4)$.  The quotient has degree at
      most 1, i.e. it is $a+b\,x$ for some $a$ and $b$
5. Worked example
   1. Substitute back: $(1-1000\,\delta) + (1+1000\,\delta) = 2$ and
      $(1-1000\,\delta) + 1.001\,(1+1000\,\delta) = 2.001 + \delta$
   2. The eigenvalues satisfy $\lambda^2 - 2.001\,\lambda + 0.001 = 0$,
      giving $\lambda \approx 2.001$ and $\lambda \approx 0.0004999$, a
      ratio of about 4000
   3. $\mat{X}$ is singular (eigenvalues 2 and 0).  If $y_1 = y_2$ there
      are infinitely many solutions, $w_1+w_2 = y_1$; otherwise there is
      no exact solution.  The ill-conditioned problem has become
      under-constrained in the $(-1,1)^\tr$ direction
