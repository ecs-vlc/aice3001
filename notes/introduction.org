** About the Course
   * The course is dual coded as AICE3001 (third year Artificial
     Intelligence) and AICE6002 (MSc Machine Learning), with slightly
     different assessment for the two
   * Notes are on Moodle and at [[https://ecs-vlc.github.io/aice3001/]]
   * *Why learn mathematics when LLMs can do so much?*  Because you need
     to be able to trust your own judgement.  LLMs are useful, but if you
     cannot check what they tell you, you are disempowered
   * Learning machines are notoriously hard to understand.  Mathematics
     lets us reason rigorously, build intuition we can rely on, describe
     the world precisely, and gives us a set of tools that turn out to be
     hugely useful
   * Mathematics is a language that takes time to learn.  Everyone can
     crack the code, but only by working with it---which is why the
     tutorials should be treated as compulsory
   * Students start from very different places.  If you are strong,
     become stronger by helping others; if you are starting from a weak
     base, judge yourself by your progress rather than by comparison
   * *The golden rule of mathematics: understand every step.*  When you
     don't understand a step, stop, break the problem down, and ask.
     Asking "stupid" questions is how everyone learns
   * The course covers calculus, vector spaces, convexity, optimisation,
     probability, statistics and information theory: the foundations of
     most of machine learning

** Vectors
   * Vectors in $\mathbb{R}^n$ are written in bold and are /column/
     vectors,
     $$ \bm{x} = \begin{pmatrix} 1.2 \\ 2.3 \\ 4.7 \end{pmatrix} $$
   * The corresponding /row/ vector is the transpose,
     $\bm{x}^\tr = (1.2, 2.3, 4.7)$.  Keeping track of rows and columns
     lets us write vector equations succinctly and unambiguously
   * The /inner product/ (dot product) of two vectors of the same length
     is $\bm{a}^\tr\bm{b} = \sum_i a_i\,b_i$.  It encodes the angle between
     them: $\bm{a}^\tr\bm{b} = \len{\bm{a}}\,\len{\bm{b}}\cos(\theta)$
   * The /outer product/ $\bm{a}\,\bm{b}^\tr$ is a matrix with elements
     $a_i\,b_j$, defined for vectors of any sizes
     #+ATTR_LATEX: :width 0.6\linewidth
     [[file:figures/vector_outer_product-13.pdf]]
   * The /Euclidean norm/ (2-norm) measures size,
     $$ \len{\bm{x}}_2 = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^n x_i^2} = \sqrt{\bm{x}^\tr\bm{x}} $$
     Other norms exist; this one is intimately tied to the inner product

** Matrices
   * An $m\times n$ matrix, $\mat{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$, has $m$
     rows and $n$ columns, and maps vectors in $\mathbb{R}^n$ to vectors in
     $\mathbb{R}^m$:
     $$ \mat{M}\,\bm{x} = \begin{pmatrix} 1.1 & -2.1 & 6.2 \\ 9.3 & 3.1 & -4.4 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 1.2 \\ 2.3 \\ 4.7 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 25.63 \\ -2.39 \end{pmatrix} $$
   * Each output element is a linear sum of the inputs,
     $b_i = \sum_j M_{ij}\, a_j$, so for any real $c$
     \begin{align*}
       \mat{M}\,(c\,\bm{a}) &= c\,(\mat{M}\,\bm{a}) \\
       \mat{M}\,(\bm{a}+\bm{b}) &= \mat{M}\,\bm{a} + \mat{M}\,\bm{b}
     \end{align*}
   * If $\mat{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell\times m}$ and
     $\mat{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ then $\mat{A}\,\mat{B}$ is the
     mapping "first apply $\mat{B}$, then $\mat{A}$",
     $\mat{A}\,\mat{B}\,\bm{x} = \mat{A}\,(\mat{B}\,\bm{x})$.  It is an
     $\ell\times n$ matrix computed by matrix multiplication (rows times
     columns)
     #+ATTR_LATEX: :width 0.75\linewidth
     [[file:figures/matrix_multiplication-13.pdf]]
   * Matrix multiplication is associative,
     $(\mat{A}\,\mat{B})\,\mat{C} = \mat{A}\,(\mat{B}\,\mat{C})$, but in
     general not commutative, $\mat{A}\,\mat{B} \neq \mat{B}\,\mat{A}$
   * A column vector can be treated as an $n\times 1$ matrix, so
     matrix-vector multiplication is a special case of matrix
     multiplication

** Transposes
   * The transpose swaps rows and columns,
     $[\mat{M}^\tr]_{ij} = [\mat{M}]_{ji}$, and
     $(\mat{M}^\tr)^\tr = \mat{M}$
   * A little book keeping shows
     $(\mat{A}\,\mat{B})^\tr = \mat{B}^\tr\mat{A}^\tr$
   * A matrix is /symmetric/ if $\mat{M} = \mat{M}^\tr$.  A $1\times1$
     matrix is always symmetric, which gives two identities we will use
     constantly:
     \begin{align*}
       \bm{a}^\tr\bm{b} &= \bm{b}^\tr\bm{a}
       & \bm{a}^\tr\mat{M}\,\bm{b} &= \bm{b}^\tr\mat{M}^\tr\bm{a}
     \end{align*}

** Determinants
   * Treat each point of a region of volume $V$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$ as a
     vector and map it by a square matrix $\mat{M}$.  The image has volume
     $V'$, and
     $$ \det(\mat{M}) = \pm\frac{V'}{V} $$
     where the sign records whether the mapping includes a reflection
     #+ATTR_LATEX: :width 0.7\linewidth
     [[file:figures/volume_mapping.pdf]]
   * Because $\mat{A}\,\mat{B}$ means "apply $\mat{B}$ then $\mat{A}$",
     volume scalings multiply:
     $\det(\mat{A}\,\mat{B}) = \det(\mat{A})\,\det(\mat{B})$
   * Determinants are only defined for square matrices; a non-square
     matrix maps between spaces of different dimension, where volumes
     cannot be compared
   * A /singular/ matrix squashes space onto a lower dimensional
     subspace, so its determinant is 0

** Multivariate Functions and Gradients
   * Machine learning uses functions $f:\mathbb{R}^n\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^m$
     heavily, and very often scalar functions
     $f:\mathbb{R}^n\rightarrow\mathbb{R}$, such as a learning machine
     $\hat{f}(\bm{x},\bm{w})$ or a loss function $L(\bm{w})$
   * A function is /linear/ if $f(c\,\bm{x}) = c\,f(\bm{x})$ and
     $f(\bm{x}+\bm{y}) = f(\bm{x}) + f(\bm{y})$
   * The /gradient/ of a scalar function
     $f:\mathbb{R}^n\rightarrow\mathbb{R}$ is the column vector of partial
     derivatives
     $$ \grad f(\bm{x}) = \begin{pmatrix} \partial f/\partial x_1 \\ \vdots \\ \partial f/\partial x_n \end{pmatrix} $$
   * It is orthogonal to the contours of $f$ and points in the direction
     in which $f$ increases fastest

** Probability
   * A /random variable/ assigns a number to the outcome of a random
     event: 0 for tails and 1 for heads is a discrete random variable; the
     temperature in Hyde Park at noon tomorrow is a continuous one
   * For a discrete random variable the /probability mass function/
     $\Prob{X=x}$ lies between 0 and 1 and
     $\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\Prob{X=x} = 1$
   * For a continuous random variable the probability of any single value
     is zero, and we use the /probability density/
     $$ f_X(x) = \lim_{\delta x\rightarrow 0}\frac{\Prob{x < X \leq x+\delta x}}{\delta x} $$
   * The /expectation/ of a function $g(X)$ is its average value,
     $$ \av{g(X)} = \sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\Prob{X=x}\,g(x) \quad\text{or}\quad \av{g(X)} = \int f_X(x)\,g(x)\,\dd x $$
   * Expectation is linear, $\av{c\,g(X)} = c\,\av{g(X)}$ and
     $\av{g(X)+h(X)} = \av{g(X)} + \av{h(X)}$, and the expectation of a
     constant is itself, $\av{c} = c$

** Expectations
   * For a fair six-sided die $X$, compute $\av{X}$, $\av{X^2}$ and the

     $\av{a\,X + b} = a\,\av{X} + b$

* Answers

** Matrix multiplication does not commute
   * $$ \mat{A}\,\mat{B} = \begin{pmatrix} 7 & 2 \\ 3 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \qquad \mat{B}\,\mat{A} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 7 \end{pmatrix} $$
     which are different

** Transpose of a product
   * $[(\mat{A}\,\mat{B})^\tr]_{ij} = [\mat{A}\,\mat{B}]_{ji} = \sum_k A_{jk}\,B_{ki}$
   * $[\mat{B}^\tr\mat{A}^\tr]_{ij} = \sum_k [\mat{B}^\tr]_{ik}\,[\mat{A}^\tr]_{kj} = \sum_k B_{ki}\,A_{jk}$
   * These are the same sum, so the matrices are equal

** Determinants as areas
   * $\det(\mat{M}) = 2\times3 - 1\times1 = 5$
   * The unit square is mapped to a parallelogram of area 5, with no
     reflection since the determinant is positive

** Expectations
   * $\av{X} = \frac{1}{6}(1+2+\cdots+6) = 3.5$
   * $\av{X^2} = \frac{1}{6}(1+4+9+16+25+36) = 91/6$
   * The variance is $91/6 - 49/4 = 35/12 \approx 2.92$
   * For a discrete variable
     $\av{a\,X+b} = \sum_x \Prob{X=x}(a\,x+b) = a\sum_x\Prob{X=x}\,x + b\sum_x\Prob{X=x} = a\,\av{X} + b$,
     using $\sum_x\Prob{X=x} = 1$ (the continuous case is identical with
     integrals)
