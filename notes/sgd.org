* Main Points

** Optimisation at Scale
   * Deep networks have millions to billions of parameters.  A Hessian
     with $10^9$ parameters would have $10^{18}$ entries, so Newton and
     quasi-Newton methods are out; we use gradients only
   * *Mini-batches.*  Rather than the gradient of the loss over the
     whole dataset $\mathcal{D}$ we use a random mini-batch
     $\mathcal{B}\subset\mathcal{D}$, $|\mathcal{B}| \ll |\mathcal{D}|$:
     $$ \grad L_{\mathcal{B}}(\bm{w}) = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}|}\sum_{(\bm{x},y)\in\mathcal{B}} \grad L(\bm{x},y|\bm{w}) $$
     Averaging (rather than summing) makes this an unbiased estimate of
     the full gradient $\grad L_{\mathcal{D}}$.  Following it is
     /stochastic gradient descent/ (SGD)
     * Each step is much cheaper, so we can take many small steps,
       and over many steps the random errors average out
   * *Automatic differentiation* computes gradients of almost any
     program, which is what made very large models practical
   * *Why the slower asymptotic convergence doesn't matter much*
     * We spend most of the time far from a quadratic minimum, where the
       asymptotic rates do not apply
     * With ReLUs the loss is not twice differentiable, so the Taylor
       argument behind Newton's method breaks down
     * We really want low generalisation error; the training loss is
       only a surrogate, so its exact minimum is not that important

** One Step Size, Many Curvatures
   * From the last lecture: along the \(i\)-th eigenvector of the Hessian a
     gradient step multiplies the error by $1-r\,\lambda_i$, so we need
     $r < 2/\lambda_{\max}$, and the flattest directions then shrink by
     only about $1-1/\kappa$ per step, with $\kappa$ the condition number
   * Too large an $r$ diverges, quickly giving NaNs.  Everything in this
     lecture is a way round this conflict

** Momentum
   * In a narrow valley gradient descent steps consistently along the
     flat direction but bounces across the steep one.  Momentum remembers
     earlier steps
     \begin{align*}
      \bm{v}^{(t+1)} &= \gamma\, \bm{v}^{(t)} - r\,\grad L_{\mathcal{B}}(\bm{w}^{(t)}) \\
      \bm{w}^{(t+1)} &= \bm{w}^{(t)} + \bm{v}^{(t+1)}
     \end{align*}
     with $0<\gamma<1$ (typically $0.9$): how much velocity is kept, so
     $1-\gamma$ acts as friction
   * Where the gradient keeps the same sign the steps add up, to an
     effective step $r/(1-\gamma)$ (ten times larger for $\gamma=0.9$,
     Exercise 3).  Where it alternates they cancel
   * *Nesterov momentum* takes the gradient where momentum is about to
     carry us,
     $$ \bm{v}^{(t+1)} = \gamma\, \bm{v}^{(t)} - r\,\grad L\bigl(\bm{w}^{(t)} + \gamma\,\bm{v}^{(t)}\bigr) $$
     * With well-tuned momentum (heavy-ball or Nesterov) the error
       factor per step on a quadratic improves from about $1-1/\kappa$
       to about $1-1/\sqrt{\kappa}$: the number of steps scales as
       $\sqrt\kappa$ rather than $\kappa$.  For $\kappa = 10^4$ that is
       roughly 100 steps per factor $e$ instead of $10\,000$
     * Convergence is still /linear/: momentum fixes the condition
       number, not the order of convergence.  Super-linear convergence
       needs curvature information (BFGS, last lecture)
     * On general convex problems Nesterov's method reaches error
       $O(1/t^2)$ after $t$ steps, against $O(1/t)$ for gradient
       descent; no method that only uses gradients can do better.  The
       optional module COMP6260, Optimisation for Machine Learning,
       covers this properly

** Adaptive Methods
   * Different parameters need different step sizes.  Adaptive methods
     rescale the step for each parameter separately, which roughly
     makes the valleys more spherical
   * *RMSProp* keeps a running average of the squared gradient for each
     parameter and divides by its square root
     \begin{align*}
       S_i^{(t+1)} &= (1-\gamma)\, S_i^{(t)} + \gamma
          \left(\frac{\partial L_{\mathcal{B}}}{\partial w_i}\right)^2 \\
       w_i^{(t+1)} &= w_i^{(t)} - \frac{r}{\sqrt{S_i^{(t+1)}}+\epsilon}\,
          \frac{\partial L_{\mathcal{B}}}{\partial w_i}
     \end{align*}
     Parameters with large gradients take small steps and those with
     small gradients large ones; each moves about $r$ per step,
     whatever the scale of the loss
   * *Adam* (adaptive moment estimation) is RMSProp plus momentum.  It
     keeps running averages of the gradient and of its square,
     $$ M_i^{(t+1)} = (1-\beta)\,M_i^{(t)} + \beta \,\frac{\partial L_{\mathcal{B}}}{\partial w_i},
        \qquad
        S_i^{(t+1)} = (1-\gamma)\,S_i^{(t)} + \gamma\left(\frac{\partial L_{\mathcal{B}}}{\partial w_i}\right)^2 $$
     * Both start at zero, so early on they are biased towards zero.
       After $t+1$ updates a constant input $x$ gives only
       $(1-(1-\beta)^{t+1})\,x$ (Exercise 1), so Adam corrects them:
       \begin{align*}
	\hat{M}_i^{(t+1)} &= \frac{M_i^{(t+1)}}{1-(1-\beta)^{t+1}}
      	&
        \hat{S}_i^{(t+1)} &= \frac{S_i^{(t+1)}}{1-(1-\gamma)^{t+1}}
       \end{align*}
     * The update is
       $$ w_i^{(t+1)} = w_i^{(t)} - \frac{r}{\sqrt{\hat{S}_i^{(t+1)}} + \epsilon}\,\hat{M}_i^{(t+1)} $$
     * In this notation the usual defaults are $\beta=0.1$ and
       $\gamma=0.001$ (the literature writes $\beta_1 = 1-\beta = 0.9$
       and $\beta_2 = 1-\gamma = 0.999$)
     * Adam and its variants are very robust: a network that will not
       train with SGD often trains with Adam
   * *AdamW.*  Weight decay pulls the weights towards zero.  Adding
     $\tfrac{\eta}{2}\len{\bm{w}}^2$ to the loss adds $\eta\,w_i$ to each
     gradient, which Adam then divides by $\sqrt{\hat{S}_i}$, so weights
     with large gradients are hardly decayed at all.  AdamW applies the
     decay to the weights directly, $w_i \leftarrow w_i - r\,\eta\,w_i$,
     outside the adaptive rescaling (Exercise 4).  It is the default
     for training transformers
   * *Covariance.*  Standard vector operations (addition, scalar and
     matrix multiplication) give results that do not depend on the
     coordinate system: rotate the coordinates and you get the rotated
     answer.  Element-wise operations do not have this property
     * Adaptive methods rescale each coordinate separately, so they are
       not invariant to rotations
     * If two weights interact, the curvature can be high along
       $\bm{e}_i+\bm{e}_j$ and low along $\bm{e}_i-\bm{e}_j$.  Rescaling
       $w_i$ and $w_j$ separately cannot equalise this

** Learning-Rate Schedules
   * In practice the learning rate is changed during training
     #+ATTR_LATEX: :width 0.7\textwidth
     [[file:figures/lrSchedules.pdf]]
     * Large $r$ early: the noisy steps cross small valleys and find the
       big ones
     * Small $r$ late: settle into a valley.  Dividing $r$ by ten when
       progress stalls often gives a sudden improvement (step decay)
     * Cosine decay lowers $r$ smoothly; cyclical schedules repeatedly
       raise and lower it
     * A short /warm-up/ at the start stops the first steps diverging
       before Adam's running averages have settled; common when
       training transformers

** Loss Landscapes
   * We minimise over an enormous space.  Unless the loss is convex
     there will be many local minima, and for non-convex losses no
     efficient algorithm is guaranteed to find the global minimum.  In
     such large spaces we may never reach even a local minimum
   * Mini-batch noise means the weights keep moving; a smaller step
     averages over more batches and follows the true gradient more
     closely
   * *Symmetries*
     * Permuting the neurons of a layer (or the feature maps of a CNN),
       together with their weights, gives the same network: $n!$
       equivalent solutions for $n$ hidden units
     * For odd activations such as $\tanh$, flipping the sign of a
       unit's input and output weights also changes nothing
     * There are continuous symmetries too: in a linear network
       $\mat{W}_2\mat{W}_1 = (\mat{W}_2\mat{U})(\mat{U}^{-1}\mat{W}_1)$ for
       any invertible $\mat{U}$
     * So there are huge sets of weights with the same loss.  Between
       two equivalent minima there must be a saddle point
     * Skip connections (ResNets, transformers) break the permutation
       symmetry, which seems to make optimisation easier

* Exercises

1. *Removing the start-up bias.*  Consider a running average
   $$ a^{(t+1)} = (1-\gamma) \,a^{(t)} + \gamma \,x^{(t)} $$
   with constant input $x^{(t)} = x$ and $a^{(0)}=0$
   1. Write $a^{(t)}$ as a sum
   2. Using the sum of a geometric series,
      $$ \sum_{i=0}^{t-1} z^i = 1 + z+ \cdots + z^{t-1}= \frac{1-z^t}{1-z} $$
      write $a^{(t)}$ in closed form
   3. Find the correction that makes the corrected average equal $x$
      for all $t$
2. *Gradient descent in a quadratic minimum.*  Minimise
   $$ f(\bm{x}) = \frac{1}{2}(\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{*})^{\tr} \mat{Q} (\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{*}) $$
   using $\bm{x}(t+1) = \bm{x}(t) - r\, \grad f(\bm{x})$
   1. Write down the update equation
   2. Subtract $\bm{x}^{*}$ from both sides and write down an update
      equation for $\bm{x}(t)-\bm{x}^{*}$
   3. Write a closed form solution for $\bm{x}(t)-\bm{x}^{*}$
   4. Using the eigen-decomposition
      $\mat{Q} = \mat{V}\,\mat{\Lambda}\,\mat{V}^{\tr}$ rewrite the closed form solution
   5. Defining $\bm{u}(t) = \mat{V}^{\tr}(\bm{x}(t)-\bm{x}^{*})$ write the
      update equation for the components $u_{i}(t)$ and explain what
      happens when $\lambda_i > 2/r$
3. *Momentum adds up.*  Suppose the gradient is the same, $\bm{g}$, at
   every step.  Starting from $\bm{v}^{(0)} = \bm{0}$, find $\bm{v}^{(t)}$
   and its limit as $t\to\infty$
4. *Why AdamW?*  With the loss $L(\bm{w}) + \tfrac{\eta}{2}\len{\bm{w}}^2$
   1. Show that plain gradient descent on this loss is the same as
      gradient descent on $L$ followed by $w_i \leftarrow w_i - r\,\eta\,w_i$
   2. Explain why this is no longer true for Adam

* Experiments

** Gradient descent, momentum and Nesterov
   * On a quadratic $f(\bm{w}) = \tfrac12\bm{w}^\tr\mat{H}\bm{w}$ with a
     random positive definite Hessian, compare the methods with the same
     step size

#+BEGIN_SRC python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
X = rng.normal(size=(5, 4))
H = X.T @ X                       # Hessian of f(w) = w^T H w / 2
lam = np.linalg.eigvalsh(H)
print("eigenvalues:", np.round(lam, 2), " kappa =", round(lam[-1] / lam[0]))

f = lambda w: w @ H @ w / 2
grad = lambda w: H @ w
w0 = rng.normal(size=4)
r = 1 / lam[-1]                   # safely below 2 / lambda_max

def run(gamma, nesterov=False, steps=200):
    w, v = w0.copy(), np.zeros(4)
    for t in range(steps):
        g = grad(w + gamma * v) if nesterov else grad(w)
        v = gamma * v - r * g
        w = w + v
    return f(w)

print("gradient descent:", run(0.0))
print("momentum       :", run(0.9))
print("Nesterov       :", run(0.9, nesterov=True))
print("r = 2.1/lambda_max diverges:", end=" ")
r = 2.1 / lam[-1]; print(run(0.0))
#+END_SRC

   * Here $\kappa\approx39$.  After 200 steps gradient descent reaches
     $f\approx5\times10^{-6}$, momentum $10^{-10}$ and Nesterov $10^{-14}$;
     just over the stability limit gradient descent blows up.  Try other
     values of =gamma= and =r=

* Solutions

1. Removing the start-up bias
   1. Writing $a^{(t)}$ as a sum
      \begin{align*}
       a^{(1)} &= (1-\gamma)\, a^{(0)} + \gamma \, x = \gamma \, x\\
       a^{(2)} &= (1-\gamma)\, a^{(1)} + \gamma \, x = (1-\gamma)\,\gamma \, x + \gamma \, x\\
       a^{(t)} &= \gamma\,x\sum_{i=0}^{t-1} (1-\gamma)^i
      \end{align*}
   2. To prove the identity, multiply the series by $1-z$:
      $(1-z)(1 + z+ \cdots + z^{t-1}) = 1-z^t$.  Then
      $$ a^{(t)} = \gamma\,x\, \frac{1-(1-\gamma)^t}{1-(1-\gamma)} = x\,\bigl(1-(1-\gamma)^t\bigr) $$
      which only approaches $x$ as $t\rightarrow\infty$
   3. Divide by $1-(1-\gamma)^t$:
      $$ \bar{a}^{(t)} = \frac{a^{(t)}}{1-(1-\gamma)^t} = x $$
      Adam's $\hat{M}^{(t+1)}$ and $\hat{S}^{(t+1)}$ are this correction
      after $t+1$ updates
2. Gradient descent in a quadratic minimum
   1. $\bm{x}(t+1) = \bm{x}(t) - r\,\mat{Q} (\bm{x}(t)-\bm{x}^{*})$
   2. $\bm{x}(t+1) - \bm{x}^{*} = (\mat{I}- r\,\mat{Q}) (\bm{x}(t)-\bm{x}^{*})$
   3. $\bm{x}(t) - \bm{x}^{*} = (\mat{I}- r\,\mat{Q})^t (\bm{x}(0)-\bm{x}^{*})$
   4. $\bm{x}(t) - \bm{x}^{*} = \mat{V}(\mat{I}- r\,\mat{\Lambda})^t\mat{V}^{\tr} (\bm{x}(0)-\bm{x}^{*})$,
      because $\mat{I}- r\,\mat{Q} = \mat{V}(\mat{I}-r\,\mat{\Lambda})\mat{V}^{\tr}$
      and in its \(t\)-th power every inner $\mat{V}^{\tr}\mat{V}$ is $\mat{I}$
   5. $u_i(t) = (1-r\,\lambda_i)^t\, u_i(0)$.  If $\lambda_i > 2/r$ then
      $|1-r\lambda_i|>1$ and $u_i(t)$ grows exponentially: any component
      along $\bm{v}_i$ diverges if the curvature in that direction
      exceeds $2/r$
3. $\bm{v}^{(1)} = -r\bm{g}$, $\bm{v}^{(2)} = -r\bm{g}(1+\gamma)$, and in
   general $\bm{v}^{(t)} = -r\,\bm{g}\,(1-\gamma^t)/(1-\gamma)$, which
   tends to $-r\,\bm{g}/(1-\gamma)$: an effective step size $r/(1-\gamma)$
4. Why AdamW
   1. The gradient of the penalty is $\eta\,\bm{w}$, so the step is
      $\bm{w} - r\,\grad L - r\,\eta\,\bm{w}$: a gradient step on $L$ plus
      the decay $w_i \leftarrow w_i - r\,\eta\,w_i$
   2. Adam divides each component of the gradient, including
      $\eta\,w_i$, by $\sqrt{\hat{S}_i}$ (and averages it into the
      momentum).  Weights with large gradients get large $\hat{S}_i$ and
      so very little decay.  AdamW decays every weight by the same
      fraction $r\,\eta$
